# Computing Diffusion Geometry

Iolo Jones University of Oxford
David Lanners Durham University

## Abstract

Calculus and geometry are ubiquitous in the theoretical modelling of scientific phenomena, but have historically been very challenging to apply directly to real data as statistics.
Diffusion geometry is a new theory that reformulates classical calculus and geometry in terms of a diffusion process, allowing these theories to generalise beyond manifolds and be computed from data.
This work introduces a new computational framework for diffusion geometry that substantially broadens its practical scope and improves its precision, robustness to noise, and computational complexity.
We present a range of new computational methods, including all the standard objects from vector calculus and Riemannian geometry, and apply them to solve spatial PDEs and vector field flows, find geodesic (intrinsic) distances, curvature, and several new topological tools like de Rham cohomology, circular coordinates, and Morse theory.
These methods are data-driven, scalable, and can exploit highly optimised numerical tools for linear algebra.

# 1 Introduction

[anchor: S1.SS1]
## 1.1 The problem of data-driven calculus

Calculus is the mathematical language for describing change.
It is a foundational method in many areas of science, where objects like functions and vector fields describe all sorts of phenomena, and calculus methods like derivatives and differential equations are used to study them.
For example, a wave can be modelled by a function which measures its height at each point, and its movement is described by the wave equation.

Calculus can measure change through time, but also change through space.
When this space is curved, the derivatives will become deformed and affect the behaviour of the calculus.
For example, an electrical wave travelling across the surface of the brain will speed up and slow down as it moves in and out of bumps along the surface.
In this way, the calculus of curved spaces implicitly encodes something about their shape, and the ways in which this geometry can be inferred from calculus are studied in Riemannian geometry.

Despite the great significance of calculus as a theoretical tool for modelling dynamics and measuring geometry, it has historically been very difficult to apply directly to data.
What happens when we only have a finite sample of points from a space, and want to estimate its calculus?
We might have an MRI scan of a brain and want to study how its shape will affect the propagation of electrical waves across the surface.

There are two major obstacles.
First, calculus involves taking derivatives, which are the infinitesimal rates of change measured by ‘zooming in’ infinitely closely to a point.
Unlike in continuous space, finite data are all isolated, and the gaps between them prevent any infinitesimal measurements.
Second, the classical theory of Riemannian geometry only applies when the space is a manifold.
Manifolds are highly specialised spaces that satisfy very specific constraints.
Most real data do not meet these constraints (as we discuss below), and so, even if we could compute it, the classical Riemannian geometry would rarely apply in practice.

Diffusion geometry 5152 is a new theory that simultaneously overcomes both of these obstacles by reformulating calculus and geometry in terms of the heat diffusion on the underlying space.
Heat diffusion (as described by the classical heat equation) may appear to be an extremely specific dynamical process and unrelated to the other calculus objects, but, remarkably, it contains enough information to fully reconstruct all the calculus and geometry of the space.

This has two important consequences.
First, the heat diffusion can be expressed using the heat kernel, which measures how heat spreads from one point to another over time.
We can evaluate the heat kernel on finite data to get a discrete approximation of the diffusion process, which can then be used to compute calculus objects using their reformulation in diffusion geometry.
The heat kernel is easily computable and highly robust to noise, and all our methods will inherit these qualities.
Second, the heat diffusion can be defined on very general spaces, not just manifolds.
This means that diffusion geometry and the methods we derive from it will apply to all real-world data geometries, without requiring special assumptions.

[anchor: S1.SS2]
## 1.2 Why the “manifold hypothesis” has the wrong name

A space must satisfy several strict criteria to be a manifold.
It must have a dimension d, meaning that, at every point, the space locally ‘looks like’ the standard d-dimensional space ℝᵈ.
As such, it cannot have locally variable dimension (like a line, which is 1d, joined to a sphere, which is 2d) or any tree-like ‘branching points’ where different parts of the space split off.
While these criteria are met in special cases, most real-world data have branches and variable dimensions, so are not manifolds (see Figure 4).
On the other hand, a fundamental observation in data science is that real-world data tend to have low-dimensional structure.
For example, the space of natural images is intrinsically low-dimensional because, given some initial image, there are only certain ‘directions’ in which you can change that image and obtain another natural image (you could safely change the colour of the background, but changing pixel values randomly just looks like noise).

Confusingly, the reasonable and broadly applicable assumption that the data are low-dimensional has been mislabelled the “manifold hypothesis”, and the low-dimensional data are called “data manifolds” 22.
In fact, the two properties are unrelated.
For example, three lines that meet at a single point (like a letter ‘Y’) form a 1-dimensional space that is not a manifold, and a 999,999-dimensional sphere in 1,000,000-dimensional space is a manifold, but is not low-dimensional.

The goal of diffusion geometry is to redefine Riemannian geometry, which only exists on actual manifolds, so that the same methods can be applied to the things that people mean when they say “data manifold”.

However, there are several practical drawbacks to this approach that we discuss in Section 10.
This paper introduces a new computational framework based on a simple and general recipe directly involving the carré du champ operator 8 from the theory of diffusion geometry.
This leads to a much simplified mapping from theory to computation, as well as improved performance and scalability.
It provides a general, robust approach to data-driven Riemannian geometry, which can be exploited for statistics and machine learning.

In Section 2, we briefly illustrate the main ideas of diffusion geometry and demonstrate our computational approach by computing gradient vector fields on point clouds.
The central objects of calculus and geometry are functions, vector fields, differential forms, and tensors: in Section 3, we describe a framework in which they can be represented and manipulated.
In Section 4, we provide a theoretical justification for the approach, showing that it is well-posed and numerically stable.
The interesting information in calculus and geometry is captured by differential operators that map between these spaces, such as the exterior derivative and Lie bracket, and these are described in Section 5.

We can use this framework to compute the calculus, geometry, and topology of point clouds with diffusion geometry.

## 2.1 Vector calculus on ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} and manifolds

A fundamental object in vector calculus is a vector field, which is an assignment of a ‘direction’ to each point in space.
We can represent this direction at a point p∈ℝᵈ by the vector (f₁(p),...,fd(p))∈ℝᵈ, which is expressed by the notation
    X=Σᵢ₌₁ᵈfᵢ∇ xᵢ.

The term ∇ xᵢ refers to the vector field eᵢ=(0,...,1,...,0), which represents a constant flow with speed 1 in the direction of the iᵗʰ axis.

In geometry, we traditionally work with manifolds, which we can think of as subsets M⊆ℝᵈ that meet certain criteria.
A manifold must have a dimension d′≤ d, which means that, at every point p∈M, the space M locally ‘looks like’ ℝᵈ′.
This is encoded in the tangent space TₚM, which is a d′-dimensional vector space that contains all the infinitesimal directions through which a curve in M can pass through p.
We can define vector fields on M by projecting them from ℝᵈ onto TₚM at each point p, a process which is called the pullback.
We can use the same notation as Equation (1), where fᵢ now means a function M→ℝ, and ∇ xᵢ means the projection of eᵢ to TₚM.

## 2.2 Diffusion geometry on ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} and manifolds

The two aims of diffusion geometry are to generalise the above constructions to more general spaces than manifolds, and to do so in a way that can be computed from data.
These goals share a common solution, which is to reformulate the classical theory in terms of a diffusion process.

In our definition of vector fields on manifolds, we used the pointwise projection of an ambient vector field onto the tangent space TₚM, and this projection is defined using the pointwise inner product.
More generally, the pointwise inner product X· Y of two vector fields (which defines a function) is a fundamental construction in calculus.
This idea is abstracted by the theory of Riemannian geometry, which is formulated entirely in terms of the pointwise inner product, called the Riemannian metric and denoted g(X,Y).
Diffusion geometry generalises this further by noting that, if X=Σᵢ₌₁ᵈfᵢ∇ xᵢ and Y=Σⱼ₌₁ᵈhⱼ∇ xⱼ, then
    g(X,Y)=Σᵢ,ⱼ₌₁ᵈfᵢhⱼg(∇ xᵢ,∇ xⱼ),

where the terms g(∇ xᵢ,∇ xⱼ) are called the carré du champ 8 of xᵢ and xⱼ, denoted Γ(xᵢ,xⱼ).
The carré du champ can be expressed in terms of a diffusion process on the manifold M, but, crucially, can also be defined using more general diffusions on more general spaces.
By generalising the diffusion, we can generalise the carré du champ, and so generalise the geometry.

The specific diffusion process that captures the geometry of Euclidean space ℝᵈ and manifolds M is the heat flow.
On ℝᵈ, it is defined by the heat kernel
    pₜ(x,y)=1/(4π t)⁽ᵈ⁄²⁾exp(-x-y²/4t)

for t>0 and x,y∈ℝᵈ, which we can interpret as the probability of a Brownian motion transitioning from x to y after t time.
For our purposes, the important geometric information is summarised by the following proposition, which relates the heat kernel to the carré du champ.

## 2.3 Computing the carré du champ

The covariance formula (2) relates the carré du champ Γ to the heat kernel.
We can compute a data-driven carré du champ by first computing the kernel pₜ on the data and then applying the same formula.
Given n data points in ℝᵈ, we evaluate pₜ(xᵢ,xⱼ) for all i,j=1,...,n, to obtain an n× n matrix.
We row-normalise to obtain a matrix P whose rows sum to 1, so P defines a discrete diffusion process or Markov chain.
A function f on the data can be represented by a vector f∈ℝⁿ, i.e. fᵢ=f(xᵢ), and we can apply (2) to compute the discrete carré du champ Γ(f,h)∈ℝᵈ as
    Γᵢ(f,h)=1/2tΣⱼ₌₁ⁿPᵢⱼ(fⱼ-fᵢ)(hⱼ-hᵢ).

## 2.4 Gradient vector fields

As a simple application of our carré du champ formula, we can compute the gradient ∇ f of a function f, which is a vector field measuring the speed and direction in which f is increasing the fastest.
The gradient is inherently spatial, because it measures the variation of f with respect to the underlying geometry.

We can visualise any vector field X by taking the derivative of each coordinate function xᵢ in the direction of X, and plotting the ‘arrow’ given by these derivatives (X(x₁),...,X(xd))∈ℝᵈ at each point.
This derivative is given by the Riemannian metric (i.e. pointwise inner product), because X(h)=g(X,∇ h) for any function h.
When the vector field X is ∇ f, this is a straightforward computation with the carré du champ
    ∇ f(x₁)
    ...
    ∇ f(xd)
    =g(∇ f,∇ x₁)
    ...
    g(∇ f,∇ xd)
    =Γ(f,x₁)
    ...
    Γ(f,xd)

which we can compute using (3).
So, at the iᵗʰ point in the data, we get a vector
    (Γᵢ(f,x₁),...,Γᵢ(f,xd))∈ℝᵈ.

In Figure 4, we verify that this carré du champ gradient computation aligns with the ground truth when computed from a sparse sample of data from a manifold (second and third columns).
However, this method does not assume that the data lie on a manifold.
We can directly apply it to non-manifold data that has noise, variable density, variable dimension, and singularities, and get meaningful results (fourth column).

# 3 Functions, vector fields, forms, and tensors

The fundamental objects of calculus and geometry are tensors, such as functions, vector fields, and differential forms.
These can all be computed using the carré du champ Γ constructed from a Markov chain on the data, as demonstrated above.

[anchor: S3.SS1]
## 3.1 Carré du champ and measure from a Markov chain

The ‘geometry’ in diffusion geometry is derived from a diffusion process on the space M, which provides us with a measure μ and a carré du champ operator Γ.
When we have a finite point cloud M=p₁,...,pₙ⊂ℝᵈ, we can approximate the diffusion process on M by constructing a Markov chain on M.
However, the computational methods that follow would work for any choice of measure and carré du champ on M, which could be constructed in different ways depending on the application.

[anchor: S3.SS1.SSS1]
### 3.1.1 Constructing a Markov chain from a kernel

An easy way to construct a Markov chain from data is to use a kernel k:ℝᵈ×ℝᵈ→ℝ₊, which measures the ‘affinity’ between a pair of points.
A standard choice is the gaussian kernel
    k(x,y)=exp(-∥ x-y∥²/t²)

for some bandwidth parameter t>0.
The bandwidth t sets the length scale of the kernel and needs to be tuned so that the Markov chain will encode the local geometry of the data.
When t is too small, the kernel can only ‘see’ the point on which it is centred, and the Markov chain becomes the identity matrix, failing to encode any of the relationships between points.
When t is too large, the kernel is too smoothed out, and the Markov transition probabilities become uniform, missing the subtle details.
In practice, it is standard to use a variable bandwidthkernel
    k(x,y)=exp(-∥ x-y∥²/ρ(x)ρ(y))

for some bandwidth function ρ:ℝᵈ→ℝ₊.
These lead to better performance on variable-density and heterogeneous data, because the bandwidth can be increased at low-density points (where the gaps between data are larger) and decreased at high-density points (to capture the finer details).

Although finding a good bandwidth function ρ is essential for the performance of the kernel, there are simple heuristics that attain very good results, such as setting ρ(pᵢ) to be the distance to the kᵗʰ nearest neighbour of pᵢ (usually k=8).
In our implementation, we closely follow 18 in constructing a local density estimate q(x), and then set ρ(x)=q(x)ᵇ for some negative power b.

We can obtain a Markov chain from k by computing the kernel matrix and then row-normalising:

[anchor: S3.I1]
[anchor: S3.I1.i1]
Let Kᵢⱼ=k(pᵢ,pⱼ) be the n× n kernel matrix.

[anchor: S3.I1.i2]
Let Dᵢ=Σⱼ₌₁ⁿKᵢⱼ be the row sums (so D∈ℝⁿ).

[anchor: S3.I1.i3]
Define a Markov chain Pᵢⱼ=Kᵢⱼ/Dᵢ.


Computing the whole kernel matrix K takes O(n²d) time and O(n²) space, which is infeasible for large datasets.
To avoid this, we can compute only the k nearest neighbours of each point (for some small k, usually k=32), and set Kᵢⱼ=0 for all other pairs (i,j).
This leads to a sparse kernel matrix with only O(kn) nonzero entries, and costs only O(nlog n+d) time and O(kn) space to compute using a KD-tree.

[anchor: S3.SS1.SSS2]
### 3.1.2 Measure

The vector D can be interpreted as an estimate of the density of the underlying measure μ from which the data points M were sampled.
For example, if the points in M are drawn from a distribution with density q, then for large n,
    Dᵢ/n=1/nΣⱼ₌₁ⁿk(pᵢ,pⱼ)≈∫Mk(pᵢ,y)q(y)dy

is a kernel density estimate of the function q.
We can normalise D into a probability measure (i.e. its entries sum to 1) by setting
    μ=D/Σₖ₌₁ⁿDₖ∈ℝⁿ,

which will play the role of the measure μ in our computations.

## 3.2 Functions

In the theory of diffusion geometry 51, the most fundamental objects are the measure space M and the functions f:M→ℝ.
We call the space of functions A, and A is an algebra: it is a vector space that also has a notion of multiplication (because, if f,h∈A are functions, then so is fh∈A).

Given a finite dataset M, we could define a discrete function space A as the space of all functions on M.
We would then identify A=ℝⁿ, where a function f:M→ℝ corresponds to the vector (f(p₁),...,f(pₙ))∈ℝⁿ, and function multiplication is just componentwise multiplication of vectors.
However, while ℝⁿ contains all the functions that can possibly be represented on the data, it has dimension n, and so the time and memory complexity of downstream computations will gain a compounding factor of at least O(n).
To improve this, we would like to restrict to a lower-dimensional subspace.

[anchor: S3.SS2.SSS1]
### 3.2.1 Compressed function space

There are many choices of subspace, and the properties of the functions they contain represent an inductive bias.
A natural and general choice is to pick a subspace of the smoothest possible functions, partly because smoothness is an often desirable property, and partly because the smoothness of the function controls the numerical error of the carré du champ (see Theorem 3.1 in 52).
By restricting to the smoothest possible subspace, we are therefore considering only the functions on which our methods are the most precise.

The notion of ‘smoothness’ we use will be data-driven: we can use the Markov chain P to define an energy functional
    E(f):=f,PfL²₍ℝⁿ,μ₎/fL²₍ℝⁿ,μ₎²=Σᵢ,ⱼ₌₁ⁿfᵢfⱼPᵢⱼμᵢ/Σᵢ₌₁ⁿfᵢ²μᵢ,

where 0≤ E(f)≤ 1 measures the smoothness of f with respect to P.
Since P is a diffusion operator, 1-E is a discrete analogue of the Dirichlet energy.
If n₀ is our desired subspace dimension, the n₀ orthonormal functions in L²(ℝⁿ,μ) that maximise E are precisely the first n₀ eigenvectors of the n× n matrix P.
The first eigenfunction φ₁ will equal 1 everywhere, and the other φᵢ will get progressively more oscillatory as i increases.
See the left column of Figures 5 and 6.

Since P was constructed from a symmetric kernel, it is self-adjoint with respect to the inner product on L²(ℝⁿ,μ).
This makes E a symmetric quadratic form which can be efficiently diagonalised, and the eigenvectors φ₁,...,φₙ of P form an orthonormal basis of L²(ℝⁿ,μ).
We define the compressed function space
    A=Spanφ₁,...,φₙ₀⊆ L²(ℝⁿ,μ),

and store the n₀ basis functions in an n× n₀ matrix U.
Since the eigenfunctions φᵢ minimise a Dirichlet energy, we can think of A as a space of bandlimited functions on the data.
The fact that the functions are orthonormal with respect to μ means that Uᵀdiag(μ)U=Iₙ₀.

Computing the first n₀ eigenvectors of P is a symmetric eigenvalue problem, which can be solved efficiently using iterative methods.
Since we construct a sparse P, this costs just O(nn₀) time and O(nn₀) space.

If the bandwidths in the kernel are constant, then the eigenfunctions φᵢ converge to the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a manifold as n→∞ 122678471342.
A slightly different normalisation is required to get this convergence when using variable bandwidths 18, which ensures that the resulting eigenfunctions do not depend on the sampling density of the data.
We do not use that normalisation here and directly diagonalise the Markov chain, based on the heuristic that incorporating the density into the basis choice is a more data-driven approach.

[anchor: S3.SS2.SSS2]
### 3.2.2 Projection into the subspace

We can use the matrix U and measure μ to project functions f∈ℝⁿ into the compressed function space.
If f∈ℝⁿ is a function, then
    f*:=Uᵀdiag(μ)f∈ℝⁿ₀

contains the coefficients of the n₀ eigenfunctions that best approximate f with respect to the squared L²(ℝⁿ,μ) norm.
If n₀=n, then this is just a change of basis, but we will usually take n₀≪ n so the projection is into a proper subspace, resulting in an approximation
    f≈Σᵢf*ᵢφᵢ=Uf*∈ℝⁿ.

We can think of f* as the bandlimited Fourier transform of f.

One cost of using a compressed function space is that function multiplication is no longer closed in A.
If f,h∈A, we can compute their product f⊙h∈ℝⁿ by componentwise multiplication and then projecting back into A as (f⊙h)*, i.e.
    (φᵢφᵢ′)ₖ=Σₚ₌₁ⁿUₚₖ(UₚᵢUₚᵢ′)μₚ

for k=1,...,n₀, but this generally loses information when n₀<n.
In other words, A is a subspace of L²(ℝⁿ,μ), but not a subalgebra.

In this work, we will always evaluate function products using (8), which costs O(nn₀m) time to compute the product of m pairs of functions.
An alternative approach, used in Spectral Exterior Calculus (SEC) 16, is to precompute the n₀× n₀× n₀ tensor of function products cᵢᵢ′ₖ (8) in O(nn₀³) time, which allows m function products to be computed in O(n₀³m) time.
This gives a faster multiplication when n and m are both greater than O(n₀²).
This trade-off is justified in the SEC, where it is used extensively in backend computation to approximate expressions in the eigenfunction basis (so m is very high), but this leads to a compounding projection error.
In this work, we never perform backend computation with these expansions, and so this error does not arise.

## 3.3 Vector fields

Formally, vector fields in diffusion geometry are defined as linear combinations
    X=Σ fᵢ∇ hᵢ

for some functions fᵢ and hᵢ.
Instead of working with arbitrary functions hᵢ, we will restrict to the coordinate functions x₁,...,xd:M→ℝ defined by the ambient embedding of the data in ℝᵈ, as in Section 2.
In general, we can use any coordinates x₁,...,xd:M→ℝ that define an immersion (x₁,...,xd):M→ℝᵈ.
We will suppose that an arbitrary vector field X can be written as a sum
    X=Σⱼ₌₁ᵈfⱼ∇ xⱼ,

where xⱼ is the jᵗʰ coordinate function.
In order to compute vector fields, we will discretise the coefficient functions using n₁≤ n₀ basis functions from A, to get
    X=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼφᵢ∇ xⱼ,

so vector fields can be represented by the n₁× d coefficient matrices (Xᵢⱼ)ᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ d.
We flatten these into vectors of length n₁d, and so will think of the space of vector fields X(M) as being represented by X(M)=ℝⁿ₁ᵈ.
This choice of spanning set is illustrated for 2d data in Figure 5 and for 3d data in Figure 6.

Figure 5: Spanning sets for functions and vector fields in 2d.
We can use eigenfunctions ϕi\phi_{i} of the Markov chain as the smoothest possible function basis (left column).
The two ambient coordinates xx and yy are an embedding of the data, so we can use their gradients ∇x\nabla x and ∇y\nabla y to construct a spanning set for the space of vector fields by multiplying them by basis functions (middle and right columns).

We generally set n₁=n₀, so that the coefficient functions can be just as complex as the functions in A, but setting n₁<n₀ would save computation and memory.
By using functions from A as coefficients, the space of vector fields inherits the inductive bias encoded by the choice of function space basis.

The role of the coordinate functions x₁,...,xd is just to generate a spanning set for the space of vector fields as a module over the function algebra A, which requires that they give an immersion of the data in ℝᵈ.
However, any other set of functions that give an immersion could be used instead, and in practice we will often ‘smooth out’ the original coordinates by multiplying them by the Markov chain, i.e. using Px₁,...,Pxd, or projecting them into the compressed function space A.
If the data are very high-dimensional, a smaller set of functions obtained from a dimensionality reduction method could also be used to reduce computation and memory.
The different choices of immersion will lead to different spanning sets, which, like the choice of function space, encode an inductive bias on the vector fields.
However, they do not lead to essentially different geometries (because that depends only on the carré du champ), and so they are not equivalent to dimensionality reduction applied beforehand as data preprocessing.

### 3.3.1 Riemannian metric

The Riemannian metric defines a pointwise inner product g(X,Y) between any pair of vector fields X and Y, and is related to the carré du champ by the formula g(f∇ h,f′∇ h′)=ff′Γ(h,h′).
We can compute the metric of φᵢ∇ xⱼ and φᵢ′∇ xⱼ′ at a point p in the n× n₁× d× n₁× d 5-tensor
    gₚᵢⱼᵢⱼ′ =g(φᵢ∇ xⱼ,φᵢ′∇ xⱼ′)(p)  =φᵢ(p)φᵢ′(p)Γ(xⱼ,xⱼ′)(p)  =UₚᵢUₚᵢ′Γₚ(xⱼ,xⱼ′).

If we flatten gₚᵢⱼᵢ′ⱼ′ into an n× n₁d× n₁d 3-tensor gₚIJ then, at a fixed point p, gₚ is a symmetric n₁d× n₁d matrix that represents the metric at p.
So, if X,Y∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ are vector fields, their metric is given pointwise by (XᵀgₚY)ₚ₌₁,...,ₙ∈ℝⁿ.
The pointwise norm of a vector field X is given by X=√g(X,X), which we can compute as X=((XᵀgₚX)⁽¹⁄²⁾)ₚ₌₁,...,ₙ∈ℝⁿ.

The metric gₚ at a point p should be a positive semi-definite matrix.
Our implementation of the carré du champ (6) is guaranteed to be positive semi-definite up to numerical precision, and so the metric computed using (9) will also be positive semi-definite.

Figure 7: Riemannian metric and pointwise norm of vector fields.
The carré du champ defines a Riemannian metric (pointwise inner product) on vector fields.
We can use this to obtain the pointwise norm as ‖X‖=g​(X,X)\|X\|=\sqrt{g(X,X)}.

### 3.3.2 Inner product

The global inner product of vector fields is given by integrating the metric, i.e. X,Y=∫ g(X,Y)dμ.
We can compute the n₁d× n₁d Gram matrix of vector fields as
    GIJ=Σₚ₌₁ⁿgₚIJμₚ,

so, if X,Y∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ are vector fields, their inner product is given by XᵀGY∈ℝ.

Although (10) is a valid formula for the Gram matrix, it is unnecessarily expensive to materialise the entire n× n₁d× n₁d 3-tensor gₚIJ in memory if it has not already been computed for another purpose.
Instead, we can compute G directly from (9) in a single optimised tensor contraction.
This is a common theme that applies to all the inner product computations and many others later: most formulas for global objects are defined as the integral of pointwise objects.
By writing out the whole expression as a single tensor contraction, we can use the most optimal order of operations to save time and memory.

### 3.3.3 Visualising vector fields

As discussed in Section 2, we can visualise a vector field X∈X(M) as a collection of ‘arrows’ on the data points by computing the Riemannian metric with the ambient coordinate vector fields
    (g(X,∇ x₁),...,g(X,∇ xd))∈ℝᵈ.

If X=(Xᵢⱼ)ᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ d∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ we can compute this vector at each point p using the carré du champ as
    g(X,∇ xc)(p) =Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼg(φᵢ∇ xⱼ,∇ xc)(p)  =Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼφᵢ(p)Γ(xⱼ,xc)(p)  =Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼUₚᵢΓₚ(xⱼ,xc)

for each c=1,...,d.

## 3.4 Differential forms

Differential forms are a fundamental object in geometry and topology that provide a general theory of volumes and integrands.
We can also define them in diffusion geometry and compute with this framework.

[anchor: S3.SS4.SSS1]
### 3.4.1 1-forms

In degree 1, forms measure the lengths of curves.
In diffusion geometry, the space of 1-forms is denoted Ω¹(M) and contains the formal sums
    α=Σᵢfᵢdhᵢ,

which are ‘dual’ to vector fields.
However, 1-forms and vector fields are essentially equivalent, due to the musical isomorphism :Ω¹(M)→X(M) given by fdh↦ f∇ h, which is an isometric isomorphism.
As such, we use exactly the same representation for 1-forms as for vector fields, and write a 1-form α as a linear combination
    α=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈαᵢⱼφᵢdxⱼ,

where φᵢ is the iᵗʰ basis function of A and xⱼ is the jᵗʰ coordinate function.
We flatten the n₁× d coefficient matrix (αᵢⱼ)ᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ d into a vector of length n₁d, and so Ω¹(M) is represented by ℝⁿ₁ᵈ.
As this choice of spanning set is just the dual to the spanning set for vector fields, it is also illustrated for 2d data in Figure 5 and for 3d data in Figure 6.
Likewise, the metric on 1-forms is illustrated in Figure 7.

The fact that the isomorphism :Ω¹(M)→X(M) is isometric means that
1-forms and their dual vector fields share the same metric and inner product, i.e. g(α,β)=g(α,β) and ,β=,β.
We therefore equip the discrete space of 1-forms ℝⁿ₁ᵈ with the same metric tensor g from (9) and Gram matrix G from (10).
This makes the duality trivial, because the musical isomorphism :ℝⁿ₁ᵈ→ℝⁿ₁ᵈ is the identity map.
The inverse to [equation] is denoted :X(M)→Ω¹(M), and is also the identity map on ℝⁿ₁ᵈ.

[anchor: S3.SS4.SSS2]
### 3.4.2 Visualising 1-forms

A 1-form α can act on a vector field X to produce a function α(X), given by α(X)=g(α,X).
We can use this action to visualise 1-forms as collections of ‘arrows’ on the data points by computing
    (α(∇ x₁),...,α(∇ xd))=(g(α,dx₁),...,g(α,dxd))∈ℝᵈ

at each point.
This is exactly the same as the visualisation of the dual vector field α, as described in (11).

[anchor: S3.SS4.SSS3]
### 3.4.3 kk-forms

Forms of higher degree measure volumes in higher dimensions.
For example, 2-forms measure the area of surfaces, and 3-forms measure the volume of spaces.
We write k-forms in the space Ωᵏ(M) as the sums
    α=Σᵢfᵢdhᵢ¹…dhᵢᵏ.

Using the ambient coordinate functions, we get the expression
    α=Σ₁≤ ⱼ₁<...<ⱼₖ≤ dfⱼ₁,...,ⱼₖdxⱼ₁…dxⱼₖ=ΣJ₌₁ᵈᵏfJdx₍J₎,

where J=(j₁,...,jₖ) is a multi-index and dx₍J₎ denotes the form dxⱼ₁…dxⱼₖWe will compute k-forms by discretising the coefficient functions using n₁ basis functions from A, to get
    α=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁ΣJ₌₁ᵈᵏαᵢJφᵢdx₍J₎,

so k-forms are represented by the n₁×dk coefficient matrices (αᵢJ).
We flatten these into vectors of length n₁dk, and so identify the space of k-forms Ωᵏ(M) with ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ.
Notice that this representation of k-forms includes what was described above for k=1 as a special case.
Another special case is k=0, where the 0-forms are just functions f∈A=Ω⁰(M), which we can interpret as measuring the volume of points, i.e. signed densities.
However, if n₁<n₀, our discrete representations of A=ℝⁿ₀ and Ω⁰(M)=ℝⁿ₁ will differ, and so, to avoid confusion, we will never directly work with Ω⁰(M).
This choice of spanning set is illustrated for 2-forms on 2d data in Figure 8 and on 3d data in Figure 10.
The spanning set for 3-forms on 3d data is illustrated in Figure 9.

In this example, the local dimension of the space varies from 1 to 2, and this is measured by the 2-forms.

### 3.4.4 Riemannian metric

The metric of k-forms uses the determinant to compute a signed volume in the formula
    g(fdh₁…dhₖ,f′dh₁′…dhₖ′)=ff′det(Γ(hᵢ,hⱼ′)).

In order to compute the metric of dx₍J₎ and dx₍J′₎ for multi-indices J,J′, we note that the dk×dk matrix of determinants det[(Γ(xⱼₛ,xⱼ′ₜ)(p))ₛ∈ J,ₜ∈ J′] is precisely the kᵗʰ compound matrix of the d× d matrix (Γ(xⱼ,xⱼ′)(p))ⱼ,ⱼ′≤ d.
For each p, we can then define the dk×dk matrix
    (Γₚ(x₍J₎,x₍J′₎))J,J′≤dₖ=(g(dx₍J₎,dx₍J′₎)(p))J,J′≤dₖ

to be the kᵗʰ compound matrix of (Γₚ(xⱼ,xⱼ′))ⱼ,ⱼ′≤ d, so (Γₚ(x₍J₎,x₍J′₎))J,J′≤dₖ is a 3-tensor of shape (n,dk,dk).
Compound matrices are a fundamental object in exterior algebra (where differential forms are classically defined), and this construction of Γₚ(x₍J₎,x₍J′₎) as a compound matrix naturally includes the cases k=1, where d1=d, as well as the degenerate case k=0, where d0=1 and Γ(·,·)(p)=(1), the 1× 1 identity matrix representing the ‘metric of functions’.

We can now compute the metric of φᵢdx₍J₎ and φᵢ′dx₍J′₎ at a point p in the 5-tensor
    g⁽ᵏ⁾ₚᵢJᵢ′J′=φᵢ(p)φᵢ′(p)Γₚ(x₍J₎,x₍J′₎),

which has dimension n× n₁×dk× n₁×dk.
We flatten g⁽ᵏ⁾ into an n× n₁dk× n₁dk 3-tensor g⁽ᵏ⁾ₚII′ so, at a fixed point p, g⁽ᵏ⁾ₚ is a symmetric n₁dk× n₁dk matrix which represents the metric at p.
If α,β∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ are k-forms, their metric is given by
    (αᵀg⁽ᵏ⁾ₚβ)ₚ₌₁,...,ₙ∈ℝⁿ.

The metric on 2-forms and 3-forms is illustrated in Figure 11.

### 3.4.5 Inner product

The inner product of k-forms is given by the formula ,β=∫ g(α,β)dμ, so we can compute the n₁dk× n₁dk Gram matrix G⁽ᵏ⁾ of k-forms as
    G⁽ᵏ⁾IJ=Σₚ₌₁ⁿg⁽ᵏ⁾ₚIJμₚ.

If α,β∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ are k-forms, their inner product is given by αᵀG⁽ᵏ⁾β∈ℝ.

### 3.4.6 Wedge product

Differential forms have a notion of multiplication that extends the usual multiplication of functions (i.e. 0-forms).
If α∈Ωᵏ(M) and β∈Ωˡ(M), we can form their wedge product α∈Ωᵏ⁺ˡ(M) by
    (fdh₁…dhₖ)(f′dh₁′…dhₗ′)=ff′dh₁…dhₖdh₁′…dhₗ′.

In our discretisation, we can compute the wedge product of
    α=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁ΣJ₌₁ᵈᵏαᵢJφᵢdx₍J₎ β=Σᵢ′₌₁ⁿ₁ΣJ′₌₁ᵈˡβᵢ′J′φᵢ′dx₍J′₎

as
    α=Σᵢ,ᵢ′₌₁ⁿ₁ΣJ₌₁ᵈᵏΣJ′₌₁ᵈˡαᵢJβᵢ′J′φᵢφᵢ′dx₍J₎dx₍J′₎.

Note that the multi-indices J=(j₁,...,jₖ) and J′=(j′₁,...,j′ₗ) are in lexicographic (increasing) order, and we would like to obtain length-(k+l) multi-indices in lexicographic order as well.
However, while it is true that dx₍J₎dx₍J′₎=dx₍ⱼ₁,...,ⱼₖ,ⱼ′₁,...,ⱼ′ₗ₎, the concatenated multi-index (j₁,...,jₖ,j′₁,...,j′ₗ) is not necessarily in lexicographic order.
We therefore need to sort this list and multiply the result by the sign of the permutation, so let
    sign(J,J′)=0 J and J′ are not disjoint
    sign(π) π is a permutation of (j₁,...,jₖ,j′₁,...,j′ₗ) into lexicographic order

to give the wedge product formula
    α=Σᵢ,ᵢ′₌₁ⁿ₁ΣJ₌₁ᵈᵏΣJ′₌₁ᵈˡsign(J,J′)αᵢJβᵢ′J′(φᵢφᵢ′)dx₍π₍ⱼ₁,...,ⱼₖ,ⱼ′₁,...,ⱼ′ₗ₎₎.

As in Computational Note 3.2.2, the product (φᵢφᵢ′) is approximated in terms of the first n₁ functions from A by
    (φᵢφᵢ′)ₖ=Σₚ₌₁ⁿUₚₖ(UₚᵢUₚᵢ′)μₚ

for k=1,...,n₁.
The wedge product is defined between forms of all degrees, and so can encode a range of geometric and topological information.
See Figure 12 for several examples, as well as the function products (i.e. k=0) in Figures 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, and 13.

Recall from Computational Note 3.2.2 that our compressed function space A is not closed under multiplication, and so computing the product in A will incur some loss of detail unless n₀=n.
This limitation extends to the wedge product, because the projection (15) incurs the same loss unless n₁=n. In addition, we make use of the fact that only disjoint multi-indices contribute nonzero terms to the wedge product: whenever J∩ J′≠∅, the sign factor in (14) vanishes.
Consequently, the number of admissible index pairs is reduced from dkdℓ to dkd-kl, taking the overall computational complexity toO(nn₁dkd-kl).
This substantially decreases the effective computational cost of forming wedge products. For instance, if d=10 and k=l=3, roughly 70 of all possible pairs are immediately zero.

### 3.4.7 Visualising kk-forms

We visualised 1-forms by considering their action on the ambient coordinate vector fields, to get pointwise vectors (g(α,dxᵢ))ᵢ₌₁,...,d.
These measure 1-dimensional volumes (lengths) and so are represented by a 1-dimensional space (a direction) as well as a signed magnitude at each point (and directions with a signed magnitude are just vectors).

General k-forms measure k-dimensional volumes, so are represented by a k-dimensional subspace (a k-plane) and a signed magnitude at each point.
For example, a 2-form corresponds to a 2d plane with a signed magnitude.
We can visualise this as a disk aligned with that space and whose size varies with the magnitude, and opposite colours on either side to represent orientation.
A 3-form corresponds to a 3d volume with a signed magnitude, which we can visualise as a coloured sphere at each point, whose size varies with the magnitude and whose colour represents the sign.
To compute these objects, we generalise the approach for 1-forms to k-forms by computing, at each point, a d×…× d k-tensor whose entries are given by
    (g(α,dxⱼ₁…dxⱼₖ))ⱼ₁,...,ⱼₖ₌₁,...,d∈ℝᵈ×…× ᵈ

which is skew-symmetric in its k indices.
For example, a 2-form α will yield a skew-symmetric d× d matrix, and a 3-form β will yield a skew-symmetric d× d× d 3-tensor.

The method for computing the visualisation depends on the degree k of the form and the ambient dimension d.
In degree 1, this directly gives a collection of vectors in ℝᵈ which we can plot as arrows.
A 2-form in 2d gives a skew symmetric 2× 2 matrix
    0 a
    -a 0,

where a∈ℝ is the signed area magnitude, which we can visualise as a coloured disk of size |a| and signed colour a (see Figure 8).
A 2-form in 3d gives a skew-symmetric 3× 3 matrix which can be orthogonally diagonalised into the form
    0 a 0
    -a 0 0
    0 0 0,

where a∈ℝ is the signed area magnitude in the plane spanned by the first two eigenvectors.
We can visualise it as a coloured disk in that plane of size |a| with opposite colours on each side, depending on the sign of a (see Figure 10).
A skew-symmetric 3-form in 3d takes the form
    0 0 0
    0 0 a
    0 -a 0 0 0 -a
    0 0 0
    a 0 0 0 a 0
    -a 0 0
    0 0 0,

where a∈ℝ is the signed volume magnitude, which we can visualise as a coloured sphere of size |a| and signed colour a (see Figure 9).

## 3.5 Other tensors

Functions, vector fields, and differential forms are all examples of tensor fields, meaning they represent a tensor (in the linear-algebraic sense) attached to each point in the space.
Vector fields and 1-forms have degree 1, and so correspond to rank-1 tensors (i.e. vectors) at each point.
2-forms correspond to rank-2 tensors (matrices) that are antisymmetric.
It is precisely these constructions that we use for visualisation in Section 3.4.7.
In general, tensor algebra is a powerful framework for encoding calculus and geometric information, and we can form tensor fields of any degree.

We can define any tensor in diffusion geometry and compute it with this framework, but we focus here on 2-tensors, which can be interpreted as a square matrix at each point.
General (0,2)-tensors in the space Ω¹(M)⊗ ² are the sums
    α=Σᵢfᵢdhᵢ¹⊗ dhᵢ².

The notation (0,2) means that they correspond to a pair of 1-forms: in general, a (p,q)-tensor corresponds to p vector fields and q 1-forms.
We will compute 2-tensors in terms of the coordinate functions as
    α=Σⱼ₁,ⱼ₂₌₁ᵈfJdxⱼ₁⊗ dxⱼ₂,

where J=(j₁,j₂), and discretise the coefficient functions
    α=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₁,ⱼ₂₌₁ᵈαᵢⱼ₁ⱼ₂φᵢdxⱼ₁⊗ dxⱼ₂,

so 2-tensors are represented by the n₁× d× d coefficient 3-tensors (αᵢⱼ₁ⱼ₂)ᵢ≤ ₙ₁;ⱼ₁,ⱼ₂≤ d.
We flatten these into vectors of length n₁d², and so identify the space of 2-tensors Ωᵏ(M)⊗ ² with ℝⁿ₁ᵈ².

Figure 13: Spanning sets for symmetric 2-tensors.
We can use eigenfunctions ϕi\phi_{i} of the Markov chain as a function basis (left column).

### 3.5.1 Riemannian metric

The metric of 2-tensors is given by the formula
    g(fdh₁⊗ dh₂,f′dh′₁⊗ dh′₂)=ff′Γ(h₁,h′₁)Γ(h₂,h′₂).

We can compute the metric of φᵢdxⱼ₁⊗ dxⱼ₂ and φᵢ′dxⱼ₁′⊗ dxⱼ₂′ at a point p in the 7-tensor
    g⁽⁰,²⁾ₚᵢⱼ₁ⱼ₂ᵢ′ⱼ₁′ⱼ₂′ =φᵢ(p)φᵢ′(p)Γ(xⱼ₁,xⱼ₁′)(p)Γ(xⱼ₂,xⱼ₂′)(p)  =UₚᵢUₚᵢ′Γₚ(xⱼ₁,xⱼ₁′)Γₚ(xⱼ₂,xⱼ₂′),

which has dimension n× n₁× d× d× n₁× d× d.
We reshape g⁽⁰,²⁾ into an n× n₁d²× n₁d² 3-tensor g⁽⁰,²⁾ₚIJ so, at a fixed point p, g⁽⁰,²⁾ₚ is a symmetric n₁d²× n₁d² matrix which represents the metric at p.
If α,β∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ are 2-tensors, their metric is given by
    (αᵀg⁽⁰,²⁾ₚβ)ₚ₌₁,...,ₙ∈ℝⁿ.

### 3.5.2 Inner product

The inner product of 2-tensors is given by the formula ,β=∫ g(α,β)dμ, so we can compute the n₁d²× n₁d² Gram matrix G⁽⁰,²⁾ of 2-tensors as
    G⁽⁰,²⁾IJ=Σₚ₌₁ⁿg⁽⁰,²⁾ₚIJμₚ.

If α,β∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ are 2-tensors, their inner product is given by αᵀG⁽⁰,²⁾β∈ℝ.

We will often want to work with symmetric 2-tensors (for example, the Hessian is symmetric).
As with the alternating 2-tensors (2-forms), we can more efficiently represent these with fewer indices in the sums
    α=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₁≤ ⱼ₂αᵢⱼ₁ⱼ₂φᵢdxⱼ₁⊗ dxⱼ₂,

so 2-tensors are represented by the n₁× d(d+1)/2 coefficient matrices (αᵢJ)₍ᵢ≤ ₙ₁,J≤ d₍d₊₁₎/₂₎.

    2g⁽⁰,²⁾ₚIJ I≠ J

### 3.5.3 Action on vector fields

# 4 Frame theory and weak formulations

The spaces of functions, vector fields, forms, and other tensors provide a general framework for representing calculus and geometry objects, but much of the interesting information is contained in maps between these spaces.
For example, given some function f∈A, we would like to know its gradient ∇ f∈X(M).
Section 2 provides a direct way to visualise ∇ f by computing its action on coordinate functions, but we would really like to represent ∇ f in the spanning set for vector fields
    ∇ f≈Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼφᵢ∇ xⱼ.

This would allow us to compute more things, like its Riemannian metric with other vector fields, or the divergence of ∇ f to get the Laplacian of f.
However, the coefficients Xᵢⱼ are not immediately available from f, and depend on the choice of spanning set.

We can resolve this problem by solving an inverse problem using a weak formulation.
If ∇ f satisfies (18), we can take the inner product of both sides with each spanning set element φᵢ′∇ xⱼ′ to get
    ᵢ′∇ xⱼ′,∇ f=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼᵢ′∇ xⱼ′,φᵢ∇ xⱼ,

which is the weak formulation of (18).
If X=(Xᵢⱼ)ᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ d∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ is the vector of coefficients we want to compute, then the right-hand side of (19) is just the product G⁽¹⁾X, where G⁽¹⁾ is the n₁d× n₁d Gram matrix of vector fields defined in (10).
The left-hand side can be written using the carré du champ as
    ᵢ′∇ xⱼ′,∇ f=∫Mg(φᵢ′∇ xⱼ′,∇ f)dμ=∫Mφᵢ′Γ(xⱼ′,f)dμ,

and so easily computed from f, as discussed below.
This vector, called the load vector in numerical analysis, is denoted (∇ f)ʷᵉᵃᵏ∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ, so (19) becomes
    (∇ f)ʷᵉᵃᵏ=G⁽¹⁾X,

which we can solve for X by inverting G⁽¹⁾.
However, there are several questions of numerical stability:
[anchor: S4.I1]
[anchor: S4.I1.i1]
When can G⁽¹⁾ be inverted?

[anchor: S4.I1.i2]
When can G⁽¹⁾ be inverted stably?

[anchor: S4.I1.i3]
In practice, we will only have estimates of the Gram matrix and load vector from finite data.
Will these estimates converge to the true solution as n→∞ and n₁→∞?

We will address these in this section using frame theory 60.
However, the conclusion will be that weak formulations like (20) are well-behaved, and can be solved by standard numerical methods for rank-deficient systems.
If you are mainly interested in applying these methods, you can safely skip ahead.

## 4.1 Bessel sequences and frames for Hilbert spaces

We will address these questions in the setting of Hilbert spaces, which will simultaneously cover the infinite-dimensional continuous case of X(M), Ωᵏ(M) etc (and their Hilbert space completions L²X(M), L²Ωᵏ(M) etc), the finite-dimensional discrete analogues X(M), Ωᵏ(M) etc, and the limiting behaviour as n,n₁→∞.

The computational approach described above involves representing vectors x in a Hilbert space H in terms of a spanning set uᵢᵢ∈ℕ.
This set is called a frame if it satisfies two inequalities called the frame conditions, which guarantee that these representations are well-behaved, in the sense that the coefficients of x in the spanning set are bounded and we can solve weak formulations like (20) stably.
We consider the two frame conditions separately, starting with the most important one.

[anchor: S4.SS1.SSS1]
### 4.1.1 Bessel sequences

A crucial property of a spanning set is that, given any vector x∈ H, the sequence of inner products between x and elements of the set remains bounded.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem1]
A sequence of vectors uᵢᵢ∈ℕ in a Hilbert space H is called aB-Bessel sequence if there is a constant B>0 (called the Bessel bound) such that
    Σᵢ₌₁∞|x,uᵢ|²≤ Bx²

for all x∈ H.

We can interpret this definition as saying that the sequence of inner products with a Bessel sequence (x,uᵢ)ᵢ∈ℕ is in ℓ₂, and that the linear map x↦(x,uᵢ)ᵢ∈ℕ is bounded.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem2]
If uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is a B-Bessel sequence, then the analysis operator is the linear map T:H→ℓ₂ given by x↦(x,uᵢ)ᵢ∈ℕ, which is bounded with T≤√B.
Its adjoint T*:ℓ₂→ H is called the synthesis operator, which is also bounded with T*=T.

If T is the analysis operator of a Bessel sequence uᵢᵢ∈ℕ, then T² is the smallest B such that uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is a B-Bessel sequence.
We can easily derive a formula for the synthesis operator because, if a=(aᵢ)∈ℓ₂ and x∈ H, then
    a,Txℓ₂=Σᵢ₌₁∞aᵢx,uᵢH=x,Σᵢ₌₁∞aᵢuᵢH,

so T*a=Σᵢ₌₁∞aᵢuᵢ.
If x∈ H, we will say that a sequence a∈ℓ₂ contains the sequence coefficients of x if x=Σᵢ₌₁∞aᵢuᵢ=T*a.
As such, the analysis operator analyses an element of H by computing its inner product sequence, and the synthesis operator synthesises an element of H from such a sequence.

The fact that the sequence uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is a spanning set means that T is injective, and T* has a dense range in H.
Furthermore, the set uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is linearly dependent if and only if T* is non-injective, because they both happen exactly when there is some non-zero a∈ℓ₂ where T*a=Σ aᵢuᵢ=0.
In this case, any x∈ H can be represented by multiple coefficient sequences: if x=T*a then also x=T*(a+ker(T*)).

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem3]
If uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is a Bessel sequence in a Hilbert space H with analysis operator T and synthesis operator T*, the Gram operator is the map G=TT*:ℓ₂→ℓ₂,
which can be written
    (Ga)ᵢ=Σⱼ₌₁∞uᵢ,uⱼaⱼ

for a∈ℓ₂.

If uᵢ is finite, then G is the familiar Gram matrix.
The Gram operator G is self-adjoint, positive, and bounded with G=T²≤ B.
Since T is injective, G satisfies ker(G)=ker(T*), so is injective if and only if uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is linearly independent (so form a basis for H).

[anchor: S4.SS1.SSS2]
### 4.1.2 Weak formulations in Bessel sequences

We can use the language of Bessel sequences to describe weak formulations like (20).
In this setting, we have a Hilbert space H (e.g. the complete space of vector fields L²X(M)), and want to represent a vector x∈ H (e.g. ∇ f) as a linear combination of Bessel sequence elements.
As in (20), we will assume that we have access only to its sequence of inner products with sequence elements, i.e. Tx, and would like to recover x from that.
If a∈ℓ₂ contains sequence coefficients for x, i.e. x=T*a, then we would like to solve
    Tx=TT*a=Ga

for a.
Recall that the Gram operator G will have a non-zero kernel if the set uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is overcomplete, and so will not be invertible.
This is not really a problem, because it just means that (23) will have multiple solutions, and we only need one.
A natural choice of solution is the one with the smallest ℓ₂ norm, which is given by the following construction.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem4]
If H′ is a Hilbert space and S:H′→ H′ is a bounded self-adjoint operator, then S restricts to a bijection ker(S)⟂→ran(S), where ran(S) denotes the range of S.
The Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of S is the map S⁺:ran(S)⊕ker(S)→ H′ given by S+=(S|ker(S)⟂)−1⊕0|ker⁡(S).

The Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse S⁺ generalises the inverse, because S⁺=S⁻¹ when S is bijective.
It is densely defined because ran(S) is dense in ker(S)⟂, and generally unbounded.
To see that the solution is the smallest, notice that, if y∈ran(S) and y=Sx, then the coset x+ker(S) contains all the solutions of y=Sx.
Its minimal element is orthogonal to ker(S), and this is precisely the element obtained from S⁺.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem5]
If H′ has finite dimension n, then S can be represented by an n× n matrix S.
If S=UΣVᵀ is the singular value decomposition of S, then define Σ⁺ by inverting the non-zero singular values in Σ, and leaving the zeros as zeros.
Then S⁺=UΣ⁺Vᵀ.

In our case, we can solve the weak formulation (23) by applying the pseudo-inverse of G:ℓ₂→ℓ₂ to Tx∈ℓ₂.
If we suppose that x=T*a∈ran(T*), we know that Tx∈ran(TT*)=ran(G), so

where we use that ker(G)=ker(T*).
In this sense, the pseudo-inverse of G can guarantee a solution to a weak formulation in a Bessel sequence, which is optimal in the sense of ℓ₂ norm.

However, there are two problems.
First, the fact that G⁺ is generally unbounded means that, in practice, using it to compute solutions to (23) might be numerically unstable, as tiny perturbations could be magnified uncontrollably.
Second, the fact that ran(T*) is only dense in H means that there might be some x∈ H where Tx∉ran(G), so (23) has no solution at all.
These two problems are actually equivalent, as we now show.
First, we prove the following standard Lemma about general Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverses.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem6]
Let H′ be a Hilbert space and S:H′→ H′ be a bounded self-adjoint operator.
Then the following are equivalent:
[anchor: S4.I2]
[anchor: S4.I2.i1]
S⁺ is defined everywhere on H′,

[anchor: S4.I2.i2]
ran(S) is closed,

[anchor: S4.I2.i3]
S⁺ is bounded.


[anchor: S4.SS1.SSS2.1]
The domain of S⁺ is
    ran(S)⊕ker(S)=ran(S)⊕ran(S)⊥ ⊆ran(S)⊕ran(S)⊥ =H′,

so we see that (1) and (2) are equivalent.
To see that (2) implies (3), if ran(S) is closed then the bijective linear map S|ker(S)⟂:ker(S)⟂→ran(S) is a map between Hilbert spaces, and so the bounded inverse (open mapping) theorem ensures that (S|ker(S)⟂)−1 is bounded, and hence so is S⁺.
To see that (3) implies (2), notice that if S⁺ is bounded, then S|ker(S)⟂:ker(S)⟂→ran(S) is a bounded bijection with bounded inverse, so it is a homeomorphism.
Then ran(S)=S(ker(S)⟂) is homeomorphic to the complete space ker(S)⟂, so is itself complete and hence closed in H′.
∎

We now apply this Lemma to the Gram operator of a Bessel sequence to find the following standard properties.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem7]
Let uᵢᵢ∈ℕ be a Bessel sequence in H with Gram operator G and synthesis operator T*, and assume the span of uᵢ is dense in H.
Then the following are equivalent:
[anchor: S4.I3]
[anchor: S4.I3.i1]
G⁺ is bounded (and defined everywhere on ℓ₂),

[anchor: S4.I3.i2]
ran(G) is closed,

[anchor: S4.I3.i3]
there exists A>0 such that Ax²≤Σᵢ₌₁∞|x,uᵢ|² for all x∈ H,

[anchor: S4.I3.i4]
ran(T*) is closed (and thus equals H).


[anchor: S4.SS1.SSS2.4]
Notice that (1) and (2) are equivalent by Lemma 4.6.
To see that (3) and (4) are equivalent, we write condition (3) as Txℓ₂≥√AxH, which states that the analysis operator T is bounded below.
By the closed range theorem and the open mapping theorem, T is bounded below if and only if its adjoint T* is surjective, so ran(T*)=H.

We now show that (3) and (4) imply (2).
Condition (3) implies that ran(T) is closed, because if (Txₙ) is a sequence in ran(T) converging to some y∈ℓ₂, then (xₙ) is a Cauchy sequence in H by the lower bound on T, and so converges to some x∈ H.
By (4), ran(G)=T(ran(T*))=T(H)=ran(T) is closed.

Finally, we show that (1) implies (4).
If G⁺ is bounded, then G is bounded below on ker(G)⟂, meaning there exists C>0 such that Ga≥ Ca for all a∈ker(G)⟂.
Since G is a positive self-adjoint operator, the spectral mapping theorem implies that Ga,a≥ Ca² for all a∈ker(G)⟂.
Using the identity Ga,a=TT*a,a=T*a² and the fact that ker(T*)=ker(G), we have T*a≥√Ca for all a∈ker(T∗)⟂.
This lower bound implies that ran(T*) is closed, by applying the same argument as above in the Hilbert space ker(T∗)⟂.
∎

Applying this Lemma to the Gram operator G:ℓ₂→ℓ₂ of a Bessel sequence uᵢᵢ∈ℕ, we see that the two problems with using G⁺ to solve weak formulations are both resolved by requiring the condition (3) on uᵢᵢ∈ℕ, which we discuss next.

### 4.1.3 Frames

Corollary 4.7 motivates the following definition of a special type of Bessel sequence uᵢᵢ∈ℕ.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem8]
A sequence of vectors uᵢᵢ∈ℕ in a Hilbert space H is called an (A,B)-frame if there are constants 0<A≤ B<∞ (called the frame bounds) such that
    Ax²≤Σᵢ₌₁∞|x,uᵢ|²≤ Bx²

for all x∈ H. If A=B, the frame is called a tight frame, and if A=B=1, it is called a Parseval frame.

The lower bound condition ensures that the bijective map G|ker(T∗)⟂:ker(T∗)⟂→ran(T) has a bounded inverse, with

and so G⁺≤ 1/A.
Following Corollary 4.7, it also means that ran(T*)=H, so that every x∈ H has at least one sequence of coefficients a∈ℓ₂ where x=T*a.
In particular, we can now solve the weak formulation (23) for every x∈ H using G⁺, and the solution will be numerically stable because G⁺ is bounded.
We can quantify the stability of G⁺ through its condition number B/A, which measures the sensitivity of G⁺a to small changes in a.
We should try to pick a spanning set uᵢᵢ∈ℕ that minimises B/A, with the best possible scenario being when uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is a tight frame so B/A=1.

We can interpret the lower frame bound as forcing G to have a positive spectral gap, meaning its non-zero eigenvalues must be at least A.
Concretely, the spectrum of G must be a subset of 0∪[A,B], where 0 is the spectrum of G|ₖₑᵣ₍T*₎=0, and the spectrum of G|ker(T∗)⟂ is contained in [A,B].
Just as G is the smallest possible upper bound B for a given set uᵢᵢ∈ℕ, 1/G⁺ is the largest possible lower bound A.
As a very special case, if the spanning set forms a tight frame, then G⁺=(1/A)I on ran(G).

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem9]
In the special case that H is finite-dimensional and uᵢᵢ₌₁,...,N is a finite spanning set, uᵢᵢ₌₁,...,N is always a frame with optimal frame bounds given by the smallest and largest non-zero eigenvalues of the N× N Gram matrix G.
If H is d-dimensional then G has rank d, and so we can factorise G=PᵀΣP where P is a d× N orthonormal matrix, and Σ is a d× d diagonal matrix containing the positive eigenvalues λᵢ of G.
We can then write G⁺=PᵀΣ⁻¹P, where Σ⁻¹ is diagonal with entries 1/λᵢ.
If A is the smallest eigenvalue of G then 1/A is the largest eigenvalue (i.e. operator norm) of G⁺.

## 4.2 Frame bounds for the continuous spanning set

Before considering the discrete spanning sets for Ω¹(M), we will first analyse the frame conditions for a continuous spanning set for Ω¹(M).
Although the numerical stability concerns discussed above only apply to the discrete spanning set, we will need the following analysis of the continuous case to prove the convergence of the discrete case to the continuous case as n,n₁→∞.
We would like to know the frame bounds for the spanning sets for all the different spaces of tensors, but it will suffice to consider only the case of 1-forms, as we explain below.

We first show that the global inner product on the space of 1-forms Ω¹(M) is given by integration of the pointwise inner product g, and so testing the global frame conditions reduces to testing their pointwise equivalents.
Given a 1-form α∈Ω¹(M), we can evaluate the ℓ₂ norm in the frame condition as follows.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem10]
Suppose φᵢdxⱼᵢ∈ℕ,ⱼ≤ d is a spanning set of Ω¹(M), where φᵢ is an orthonormal basis of A=L²(μ).
Then
    Σᵢ₌₁∞Σⱼ₌₁ᵈ|ᵢdxⱼ,α|²=∫(Σⱼ₌₁ᵈg(dxⱼ,α)²)dμ

for all α∈Ω¹(M).

[anchor: S4.SS2.1]
We can rewrite
    Σᵢ₌₁∞Σⱼ₌₁ᵈ|ᵢdxⱼ,α|²=Σᵢ₌₁∞Σⱼ₌₁ᵈ|∫φᵢg(dxⱼ,α)dμ|²=Σⱼ₌₁ᵈ(Σᵢ₌₁∞|ᵢ,g(dxⱼ,α)L²₍μ₎|²).

Since φᵢᵢ∈ℕ is an orthonormal basis of L²(μ) (or more generally, a Parseval frame), this is equal to
    Σⱼ₌₁ᵈg(dxⱼ,α)L²₍μ₎²=Σⱼ₌₁ᵈ∫ g(dxⱼ,α)²dμ=∫(Σⱼ₌₁ᵈg(dxⱼ,α)²)dμ

for all α∈Ω¹(M).
∎

This lets us test the frame conditions for φᵢdxⱼ globally by testing them locally for dxⱼⱼ≤ d.
In particular, if dxⱼ satisfies the pointwise frame condition
    Ag(α,α)≤Σⱼ₌₁ᵈg(dxⱼ,α)²≤ Bg(α,α)

μ-almost everywhere for constants A and B, then integration of (25) implies the global frame condition (24).
In general, we have the following lemma.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem11]
Let A(p) and B(p) denote the smallest and largest non-zero eigenvalues of the carré du champ matrix (Γ(xᵢ,xⱼ)(p))ᵢ,ⱼ₌₁,...d at the point p.
Then α∈Ω¹(M) satisfies
    A(p)g(α,α)(p)≤Σⱼ₌₁ᵈg(dxⱼ,α)(p)²≤ B(p)g(α,α)(p)

for all p.

[anchor: S4.SS2.2]
This is a direct application of Example 4.9.
At a point p, we can view α as an element of the semi-inner product space (Ω¹(M),g(p)) (on a manifold, this is just the cotangent space Tₚ*M) which is finite-dimensional and spanned by the set dxⱼⱼ₌₁,...,d.
The gram matrix of this set is (Γ(xᵢ,xⱼ)(p))ᵢ,ⱼ₌₁,...d, and so the set forms an (A(p),B(p))-frame.
∎

We can use Lemma 4.11 to obtain the pointwise frame condition (25), and hence the global one (24), by showing that A(p) is uniformly bounded away from zero μ-almost everywhere and B(p) is uniformly bounded above μ-almost everywhere.

Notice that this Lemma also gives the conditions under which the frame bounds will be satisfied for all the different spaces of forms and tensors.
Since the Riemannian metric in these cases is given by compound matrices and tensor products formed from the base metric on 1-forms (or vector fields), its eigenvalues can also be obtained from it.
For example, the Riemannian metric on k-forms is, at each point p, given by the k-th compound matrix of the metric on 1-forms, so its eigenvalues are all possible products of k eigenvalues of the metric on 1-forms.
Thus, if the frame bounds hold for 1-forms with constants A and B, then they will hold for k-forms with constants Aᵏ and Bᵏ.
Something analogous holds for all tensor spaces, so it suffices to consider only the frame bounds for 1-forms.

[anchor: S4.SS2.SSS1]
### 4.2.1 Upper frame condition

It is easy to find an essentially uniform upper bound on B(p) in the general case.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem12]
We have B(p)≤Σᵢ₌₁ᵈΓ(xᵢ,xᵢ)∞, and so the set φᵢdxⱼ satisfies the upper bound
    Σᵢ₌₀∞Σⱼ₌₁ᵈ|ᵢdxⱼ,α|²≤ Bα²

for B=Σᵢ₌₁ᵈΓ(xᵢ,xᵢ)∞.

[anchor: S4.SS2.SSS1.1]
The eigenvalues of a positive semi-definite matrix are bounded above by the trace, so
    B(p)≤tr(Γ(xᵢ,xⱼ))=Σᵢ₌₁ᵈΓ(xᵢ,xᵢ)

for all p.
Each Γ(xᵢ,xᵢ)∈A is bounded, so B(p)≤Σᵢ₌₁ᵈΓ(xᵢ,xᵢ)∞ uniformly.
∎

[anchor: S4.SS2.SSS2]
### 4.2.2 Lower frame condition

Obtaining a lower bound on A(p) is harder.
We know that A(p)>0 for all p, but if A(p)→ 0 then the lower frame condition will fail, since it requires A(p)>A>0 μ-almost everywhere for a uniform constant A.
We will not fully address this question here, besides the following observations:
[anchor: S4.I4]
[anchor: S4.I4.i1]
If M is an isometrically embedded manifold then the eigenvalues of Γ(xᵢ,xⱼ) are either 0 or 1, so A(p)=B(p)=1 everywhere, and the set φᵢdxⱼ forms a Parseval frame.
Although we have not yet developed a theory of isometric embeddings for general Markov triples in diffusion geometry, we expect that the same will be true in the general case.
Since we almost always work with the induced metric from the ambient space, we will usually be in this situation.

[anchor: S4.I4.i2]
Suppose that M is compact with respect to some topology and the functions Γ(xᵢ,xⱼ) are continuous in that topology. Then A(p) is a continuous positive function on a compact space, so infₚA(p)>0 and the lower frame condition is satisfied.
In particular, this holds for any compact manifold, even if the embedding is not isometric.

It would be interesting to investigate and formalise these conditions in future work.
Note that, if we have an exact expression for the carré du champ operator Γ (for example, if we are working with a known manifold) instead of a data-driven approximation, then these results are sufficient to guarantee stable solutions to the weak formulation using the spanning set φᵢdxⱼ.

## 4.3 Frame bounds for the discrete spanning set

The frame bounds that matter practically are the bounds on the set φᵢdxⱼ, because this is what we actually use for computation.
Since the spaces involved are now finite-dimensional, any spanning set is automatically a frame (Example 4.9), and so the first two of the three questions posed at the start of this section are trivially true.
The important question is how the frame bounds depend on n, and whether the solution of the weak formulation (23) remains numerically stable as n increases.
We now perform the same analysis as above, swapping integrals for sums to obtain a discrete analogue of Lemma 4.10.

### 4.3.2 Lower bound

As before, obtaining a lower frame bound is more difficult.
We need to find a uniform lower bound
    Σⱼ₌₁ᵈg(dxⱼ,α)(p)²≥ Ag(α,α)(p)

that holds for all p.
Even if we had access to the exact carré du champ operator Γ and could compute the exact Gram matrix, there would still be the problem of spectral pollution when working with a finite truncation of the spanning set.
We observe this in practice, where our carré du champ matrices will almost never have exact zero eigenvalues, due to spectral pollution and the inherent uncertainty in finite data.
Even when the data are densely sampled from a compact manifold, the eigenvalues of the carré du champ matrices tend to exist on a continuous spectrum, and so there is no clear distinction between ‘zero’ and ‘non-zero’ eigenvalues.

The easy practical solution is to compute the upper bound B (the largest eigenvalue of the Gram matrix) and then pick a desired lower bound A that would ensure the condition number B/A is low enough for reasonable stability (e.g. less than 10⁵).
The idea is to restrict to the effective support of the Gram matrix, but in practice, it is just an arbitrary choice.
We can then project the spanning set onto the span of the Gram matrix eigenvectors whose eigenvalues are at least A.
This approach of spectral cutoff is discussed further below.
While it is theoretically unsatisfying, it is likely the best practical solution, because we can never know what the ‘true’ frame bounds are ‘meant’ to be when presented with finite data from an unknown source.

Fortunately, the choice of lower bound A seems to have little effect, even taking the condition number as low as 5 or as high as 10¹².
This is perhaps because, by picking a higher A (i.e. lower condition number), we exclude more ‘bad’ vectors from the space, but, if we leave them in, they have little effect anyway because their norm (square root of their eigenvalue) is low.

## 4.4 Weak formulations of operators

We can apply the method of weak formulations to compute discrete representations of linear operators as matrices (or other tensors, where appropriate).
In general terms, let A:ℝᵖ→ℝq be a linear operator represented by an unknown q× p matrix A.
Let eᵢ and fᵢ be the basis elements for ℝᵖ and ℝq respectively, and let G⁽q⁾ᵢⱼ=fᵢ,fⱼ be the Gram matrix for an inner product on ℝq.
If we can compute
    Aʷᵉᵃᵏᵢⱼ=fᵢ,A(eⱼ)=fᵢ,Aeⱼ=fᵢᵀG⁽q⁾Aeⱼ=(G⁽q⁾A)ᵢⱼ,

then we can recover A=(G⁽q⁾)⁺Aʷᵉᵃᵏ using the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse (G⁽q⁾)⁺.
This is simply solving the weak formulation for each basis vector eⱼ of ℝᵖ.
In the language of the finite-element method, G⁽q⁾ is the stress matrix and the columns of Aʷᵉᵃᵏ are load vectors.

We can recycle the same weak formulation to compute the adjoint of A.
Suppose that G⁽ᵖ⁾ᵢⱼ=eᵢ,eⱼ is the Gram matrix for an inner product on ℝᵖ, so A has an adjoint A*:ℝq→ℝᵖ.
Then
    Aʷᵉᵃᵏᵢⱼ=fᵢ,A(eⱼ)=A*(fᵢ),eⱼ=(A*)ʷᵉᵃᵏⱼᵢ,

meaning (A*)ʷᵉᵃᵏ=(Aʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀ, so A*=(G⁽ᵖ⁾)⁺(Aʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀ.

## 4.5 Convergence to continuous differential operators

In practice, we do not work with the continuous spanning set φᵢdxⱼᵢ∈ℕ,ⱼ≤ d, but rather the discrete spanning set φᵢdxⱼᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ d computed from finite data.
This is a finite-dimensional truncation of the continuous spanning set, which is approximated from finite data.
In this section, we analyse the convergence of the computed weak formulations to the true continuous operators as both the truncation size n₁ increases and the approximation error decreases.
The approximation error depends on the number of data points n and the kernel bandwidth, and so we can combine all these factors to get a convergence result in the limit of large n, small bandwidth, and large n₁.

[anchor: S4.SS5.SSS1]
### 4.5.1 Convergence of weak formulations in truncated frames

The frame condition guarantees a stable solution to weak formulations in the Hilbert space H given the entire sequence.
An important practical question is whether, if we restrict to the first k elements Uₖ=u₁,…,uₖ and solve the corresponding finite-dimensional weak formulation (23), we obtain a good approximation to the true solution as k→∞.
We show here that this is indeed the case in the noise-free setting, where the Gram matrix and load vector are computed exactly.

Let Hₖ=span(Uₖ)⊂ H be the finite-dimensional subspace spanned by Uₖ.
We saw in Example 4.9 that Uₖ is automatically a frame for the subspace Hₖ, with frame bounds Aₖ and Bₖ given by the smallest and largest non-zero eigenvalues of the k× k Gram matrix Gₖ.
We can define an approximate coefficient vector a⁽ᵏ⁾∈Kᵏ by solving the finite system
    a⁽ᵏ⁾=Gₖ⁺(Tx)ₖ,

where (Tx)ₖ=(x,u₁,…,x,uₖ)→ᵖ is the truncation of the load vector, which corresponds to
    xₖ=Σⱼ₌₁ᵏa⁽ᵏ⁾ⱼuⱼ∈ Hₖ.

The following observation guarantees that xₖ converges to x as k→∞.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem15]
The approximation xₖ is the orthogonal projection of x onto Hₖ.

[anchor: S4.SS5.SSS1.1]
For each i=1,…,k, we have
    xₖ,uᵢH=T*Gₖ⁺(Tx)ₖ,uᵢH=ₖ⁺(Tx)ₖ,Tuᵢℓ₂,

which is the iᵗʰ entry of
    (GₖGₖ⁺(Tx)ₖ)ᵢ=(GₖGₖ⁺(Tx)ₖ)ᵢ=((Tx)ₖ)ᵢ=(Tx)ᵢ=x,uᵢ

so x-xₖ,uᵢ=0.
∎

It matters here that uᵢᵢ∈ℕ is a frame for H, because then ran(T*)=H (by Proposition 4.7), so the union of the subspaces Hₖ is dense in H, and so x-xₖ→ 0 as k→∞.
This means that solving the finite matrix system associated with the first k frame elements is numerically stable (by the frame property of Uₖ) and converges to the true solution (by the frame property of the infinite sequence).

[anchor: S4.SS5.SSS2]
### 4.5.2 The problem of stable convergence in noisy truncated frames

While we know that each Uₖ is a frame for Hₖ, the weak formulation may become asymptotically unstable.
The upper bound Bₖ is well-behaved and is a non-decreasing sequence converging to B, but the lower bound Aₖ is more complicated.
The frame could be overcomplete and contain linear dependencies, but those dependencies may only appear at k=∞ (or, for practical purposes, at some very large k).
This leads to elements with very small norm for finite k, but which disappear in the limit k→∞, and so Aₖ→ 0.
This means that κ(Gₖ)=Bₖ/Aₖ→∞ as k→∞, so the finite-dimensional solutions are asymptotically unstable.

In our setting, we do not have access to the exact entries of the Gram matrix Gₖ or the load vector (Tx)ₖ, and instead compute empirical estimates Gₖ and (Tx)ₖ.
We therefore solve the perturbed system
    Gₖa⁽ᵏ⁾=(Tx)ₖ,

which leads to the error
    a-a⁽ᵏ⁾≤a-a⁽ᵏ⁾ₜᵣᵤₙcₐₜᵢₒₙ ₑᵣᵣₒᵣ+a⁽ᵏ⁾-a⁽ᵏ⁾ₐₚₚᵣₒₓᵢₘₐₜᵢₒₙ ₑᵣᵣₒᵣ.

The above analysis shows that the truncation error vanishes as k→∞, but the approximation error depends on the condition number of the Gram matrix Gₖ.
Standard perturbation theory for linear systems tells us that the relative error is bounded by the condition number κ(Gₖ)
    a⁽ᵏ⁾-a⁽ᵏ⁾/a⁽ᵏ⁾≤κ(Gₖ)/1-κ(Gₖ)δGₖ/Gₖ(δGₖ/Gₖ+δ(Tx)ₖ/(Tx)ₖ),

where δGₖ=Gₖ-Gₖ and δ(Tx)ₖ=(Tx)ₖ-(Tx)ₖ represent the approximation errors due to finite sampling and carré du champ estimation.
If the true Gram matrix Gₖ has a uniformly bounded condition number κ(Gₖ), then, provided that the perturbations δGₖ and δ(Tx)ₖ are sufficiently small, we can guarantee that the data-driven solution a⁽ᵏ⁾ converges to the true solution a⁽ᵏ⁾ as the sample size N→∞.
However, if κ(Gₖ)→∞ as k→∞, then the error bound (32) only applies when δGₖ and δ(Tx)ₖ are zero.

This is a standard problem in numerical analysis, which can be resolved using regularisation.
We replace the operator G with a modified operator Gε, where ε>0 is a small regularisation parameter, chosen so that Gε has a uniformly bounded condition number, and Gε-G→ 0 as ε→ 0.
This leads to the modified error decomposition
    a-aε⁽ᵏ⁾≤a-aεᵣₑgᵤₗₐᵣᵢₛₐₜᵢₒₙ bᵢₐₛ+aε-aε⁽ᵏ⁾ₜᵣᵤₙcₐₜᵢₒₙ ₑᵣᵣₒᵣ+aε⁽ᵏ⁾-aε⁽ᵏ⁾ₐₚₚᵣₒₓᵢₘₐₜᵢₒₙ ₑᵣᵣₒᵣ,

where the perturbation bound (32) now applies to the approximation error term and ensures that it vanishes as N→∞ for fixed n and ε.
The operator-norm convergence Gε-G→ 0 also ensures that the regularisation bias vanishes as ε→ 0.
The challenge is now to show that the truncation errors for the modified operators also vanish as k→∞, which would then ensure the convergence
    limε→ ₀limₖ→∞limN→∞a-aε⁽ᵏ⁾=0.

We will consider two regularisation methods here and discuss convergence results for both.
They both involve picking some small ε>0 and modifying the finite-dimensional weak formulation to enforce a uniform lower bound Aₖ≥ε for all k.
[anchor: S4.I5]
[anchor: S4.I5.i1]
Tikhonov regularisation replaces Gₖ with Gₖ,ε=Gₖ+εIₖ.

[anchor: S4.I5.i2]
Spectral cutoff diagonalises Gₖ and then defines Gₖ,ε by replacing any eigenvalues smaller than ε with zero.

Tikhonov regularisation is simpler to analyse and implement computationally, and has condition number κ(Gₖ,ε)≤(B+ε)/ε, but leads to a biased solution with a-aε≠ 0 for any fixed ε>0.
Spectral cutoff has several interesting advantages: it has an improved condition number κ(Gₖ,ε)≤ B/ε and produces an unbiased solution with aε=a for all ε<A.
It can also reduce the computational cost by projecting onto the smaller subspace spanned by the large eigenvectors of Gₖ.
However, showing that the truncation error aε-aε⁽ᵏ⁾ vanishes as k→∞ is non-trivial and does not hold for general frames.

We will first show, by a standard argument, that both of these regularisation methods have uniformly bounded pseudoinverses Gₖ,ε⁺≤ 1/ε, ensuring numerical stability and vanishing approximation error, and also have vanishing regularisation biases as ε→ 0.
We then recall classical results that Tikhonov regularisation has vanishing truncation error, and that, under a strong assumption, so does spectral cutoff.

[anchor: S4.SS5.SSS3]
### 4.5.3 Bias convergence and stability of spectral regularisations

Tikhonov regularisation and spectral cutoff are both examples of spectral regularisation, because the operators Gε and Gₙ,ε are obtained by applying a spectral map gε:ℝ₊→ℝ₊ to define Gε=gε(G) and Gₙ,ε=gε(Gₙ).

[anchor: S4.I6]
[anchor: S4.I6.i1]
Tikhonov regularisation uses gε(λ)=λ+ε, and

[anchor: S4.I6.i2]
spectral cutoff uses gε(λ)=λIλ>ε.


The following Lemma shows that, for a general class of spectral regularisations, the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse Gε⁺ is bounded, and the regularisation bias a-aε vanishes as ε→ 0.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem16]
Let gε:ℝ₊→ℝ₊ be a piecewise continuous function.
Define the pseudo-inverse function gε⁺(λ)=1/gε(λ) where gε(λ)≠ 0 and 0 otherwise.
Assume:
[anchor: S4.I7]
[anchor: S4.I7.i1]
gε(λ):ℝ₊→0∪[ε,∞),

[anchor: S4.I7.i2]
limε→ ₀λ gε⁺(λ)=1 for all λ>0, and

[anchor: S4.I7.i3]
there exists a constant C such that supε,λ≥ ₀λ gε⁺(λ)≤ C.

Let G=TT* be the Gram operator of a frame for a Hilbert space H, which is bounded and self-adjoint, so we can apply the spectral mapping theorem to define Gε=gε(G).
Then Gε satisfies
[anchor: S4.I8]
[anchor: S4.I8.i1]
Gε⁺≤ 1/ε, and

[anchor: S4.I8.i2]
if x∈ H, then Gε⁺Tx-G⁺Tx→ 0 as ε→ 0.


[anchor: S4.SS5.SSS3.1]
The fact that gε(λ):ℝ₊→0∪[ε,∞) means that σ(Gε)⊆0∪[ε,∞), so Gε⁺≤ 1/ε.
To prove the second claim, we apply the spectral theorem to G.
Let E be the spectral measure of G.
Let a=G⁺Tx be the true solution, which lies in ker(G)⟂=ran(G∗)=ran(G).
We can express the regularised solution as
    aε=Gε⁺Tx=Gε⁺Ga.

Notice that the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse is itself defined by a spectral map λ↦1/λIλ>₀.
Using the fact that gε(λ):ℝ₊→0∪[ε,∞), we can express Gε⁺ as the application of the spectral map
    λ↦1/gε(λ)Igε₍λ₎>₀

to G.
We can then use spectral calculus to write
    a-aε²=(I-Gε⁺G)a²=∫σ₍G₎(1-λ/gε(λ)Igε₍λ₎>₀)²dμₐ(λ).

Since σ(G)⊆0∪[A,B] and a∈ker(G)⟂ we can restrict the integral to σ(G)∖0⊂[A,B] to get
    a-aε²=∫Aᴮ(1-λ/gε(λ)Igε₍λ₎>₀)²dμₐ(λ).

The integrand is uniformly bounded for small enough ε (by the third assumption) and converges pointwise to 0 as ε→ 0 (by the second assumption), so we can apply the dominated convergence theorem to get
    ε→ ₀a-aε² =limε→ ₀∫Aᴮ(1-λ/gε(λ)Igε₍λ₎>₀)²dμₐ(λ)
    =∫Aᴮlimε→ ₀(1-λ/gε(λ)Igε₍λ₎>₀)²dμₐ(λ)
    =0

which proves the result.
∎

### 4.5.4 Truncation convergence of Tikhonov Regularisation

We now show, by a standard argument, that solutions of the truncated Tikhonov-regularised formulations converge.
Tikhonov regularisation is special because the finite-dimensional matrix Gₖ,ε=Gₖ+εIₖ is just the restriction of Gε=G+ε I to the first k components.
In other words, Tikhonov regularisation commutes with projection onto the frame, which we can exploit to prove convergence of the truncations in arbitrary frames.

Let X:ℓ₂×ℓ₂→ℝ be a bilinear form.
We say that X is bounded if |X(u,v)|≤ Muv, and coercive if X(u,u)≥ cu².
Given a bounded linear functional f, we can define an abstract weak formulation as
    X(a,v)=f(v)

for all v∈ℓ₂.
If X is bounded and coercive, then the Lax-Milgram theorem guarantees the existence of a unique solution a∈ℓ₂.
If (34) is truncated to a finite-dimensional weak formulation by evaluating v in a frame, we can recover a solution a⁽ᵏ⁾ in the span of the truncated frame.Céa’s Lemma bounds the truncation error as
    a-a⁽ᵏ⁾≤M/cinfb∈ Eₖa-b,

where Eₖ is the k-dimensional subspace generated by the first k basis elements in ℓ₂.

We can apply this to Tikhonov regularisation by setting X(a,v)=Gεa,v and f(v)=Tx,v, which gives M=B+ε and c=ε.
As we noted above, the finite-dimensional matrix Gₖ,ε=Gₖ+εIₖ is exactly the matrix representation of X restricted to the first k components, and so the solution to the matrix problem for a given k is governed by Céa’s Lemma.
The union of the subspaces Eₖ is dense in ℓ₂, so the best approximation error infb∈ Eₖaε-b converges to zero as k→∞, and hence
    limₖ→∞aε-a⁽ᵏ⁾ε=0.


[anchor: S4.SS5.SSS5]
### 4.5.5 Truncation convergence of spectral cutoff

General spectral regularisations have no guarantee of truncation convergence, because we cannot know in general whether the spectra of the finite sections Gₖ converge to the spectrum of G.
Truncation of general frames may exhibit spectral pollution, where eigenvalues of the finite sections Gₖ converge to points outside the spectrum of G.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem17]
Let G be the Gram operator of an (A,B)-frame for a Hilbert space H, and let Gₖ be its finite sections.
We say that the frame has no spectral pollution in the gap if the set of eigenvalues of Gₖ has no limit points in (0,A).

Proving that a given frame has no spectral pollution in the gap is highly non-trivial, and we will not attempt to do so here.
This assumption means that spectral cutoff regularisation has vanishing truncation error, provided that the cutoff parameter ε is chosen in the gap (0,A).
In this case, the spectral cutoff on the true operator is exact, so there is no regularisation bias, and we can show that the regularised and truncated solutions converge directly to the true solution.

[anchor: S4.Thmtheorem18]
Let G be the Gram operator of a frame with no spectral pollution in the gap (0,A), and let Gₖ be its finite sections (extended by zero to ℓ₂).
Fix ε∈(0,A), and let a=G⁺Tx be the true solution for some x∈ H, and let
    aε⁽ᵏ⁾=Gₖ,ε⁺(Tx)ₖ

denote the finite-dimensional spectral-cutoff solution, where Gₖ,ε⁺ is obtained by applying the spectral map hε(λ)=λ⁻¹Iλ>ε to Gₖ.
Then
    limₖ→∞aε⁽ᵏ⁾-a=0.


[anchor: S4.SS5.SSS5.1]
Since σ(G)⊂0∪[A,B] and ε<A, we have hε(G)=G⁺, so a=G⁺Tx=hε(G)Tx.
By the assumption of no spectral pollution, the eigenvalues of Gₖ have no limit points in (0,A).
If we pick δ with ε<δ<A, then there exists an N∈ℕ such that
    σ(Gₖ)∩[ε,δ]=∅

for all k≥ N.
We also have σ(G)∩[ε,δ]=∅ because σ(G)⊂0∪[A,B].
We now let f∈ C([0,B]) be the continuous function such that
    f(λ)=0 for 0≤λ≤ε, f(λ)=1/λ for λ≥δ,

and which is linear on [ε,δ] connecting (ε,0) to (δ,1/δ).
Then for all λ∈σ(G)∪σ(Gₖ) and all k≥ N we have f(λ)=hε(λ), so by spectral calculus
    f(G)=hε(G)=G⁺ f(Gₖ)=hε(Gₖ)=Gₖ,ε⁺

for all k≥ N.
Let y=Tx∈ℓ₂, and let Pₖ be the orthogonal projection onto the first k coordinates, so (Tx)ₖ=Pₖy.
For k≥ N we have
    aε⁽ᵏ⁾=Gₖ,ε⁺(Tx)ₖ=f(Gₖ)Pₖy a=G⁺Tx=f(G)y

so
    aε⁽ᵏ⁾-a=f(Gₖ)Pₖy-f(G)y≤f(Gₖ)(Pₖ-I)y+(f(Gₖ)-f(G))y.

The operators f(Gₖ) are uniformly bounded by 1/δ because f≤ 1/δ on [0,B], so
    aε⁽ᵏ⁾-a≤1/δ(Pₖ-I)y+(f(Gₖ)-f(G))y.

The first term tends to zero because Pₖ→ I strongly.
Since Gₖ→ G strongly and f is continuous, the continuity of functional calculus for bounded self-adjoint operators means f(Gₖ)→ f(G) strongly, so the second term also tends to zero.
Therefore aε⁽ᵏ⁾-a→ 0 as k→∞.
∎

### 4.5.6 Towards overall convergence results

In this section, we have shown that the solutions to suitably regularised solutions to weak formulations will converge to the true solutions as long as the entries of the relevant matrices and vectors converge.
This reduces the problem to the question of convergence for the carré du champ and its associated integral expressions.
For example, the entries in our Gram matrices and load vectors are of the form
    ᵢdxⱼ,α=∫φᵢg(dxⱼ,α)dμ

for some 1-form α, and it remains to show that these converge.

Although the computational framework here applies to any carré du champ estimator, the approach we laid out in Section 3 uses heat kernel estimates, for which we have several convergence guarantees in the special case of manifolds.
Our estimates involve several steps:
[anchor: S4.I9]
[anchor: S4.I9.i1]
Eigenfunction estimation: we estimate the eigenfunctions φᵢ by diagonalising the heat kernel Markov chain. This is essentially diffusion maps eigenfunction estimation 26, and requires spectral convergence of the Markov chain 752037137670.

[anchor: S4.I9.i2]
Carré du champ estimation: we estimate the inner products g(xⱼ,α) using the carré du champ operator, which only requires pointwise convergence of the Markov chain.
See Theorem 3.1 in 52 for an example result: such results are easily inherited from pointwise convergence of the Markov chain 181445.
An essential point is that we can only reliably estimate the carré du champ of functions that are sufficiently smooth.
Like most methods for discrete geometry, we use local inter-point variability as a proxy for the derivative, and if the function varies too quickly, this is not a good estimate.

[anchor: S4.I9.i3]
Monte Carlo integration: we estimate the integrals using a weighted sum over the data points.
This is easily justified by uniform convergence of the carré du champ estimates, but can be justified under weaker conditions depending on the carré du champ convergence rates and the empirical measure estimation.


However, there is a significant gap between theory and practice in the use of these methods.
First, the whole motivation for diffusion geometry is its use on general data sets, which may not lie on a manifold, but nothing is known about the above convergence in this setting.
Second, in practice, diffusion methods are usually constructed with variable bandwidth kernels, and even for manifold data, we only have formal guarantees of pointwise convergence of the Markov chain 18, and do not currently know whether the eigenfunctions converge correctly.
Third, the spectral cutoff regularisation method we proposed is common in numerical analysis (for example, it is used in Spectral Exterior Calculus 16), but is only justified under the strong assumption of no spectral pollution in the gap.

|  | theoretically justified | used in practice |
| sample space | manifold | general geometry |
| kernel bandwidths | fixed | variable |
| regularisation | Tikhonov | spectral cutoff |

The lag of theory behind practice presents an open problem, where empirical performance (such as on the non-manifold examples in this paper) demonstrates that much stronger theoretical results could be obtained than those currently known.
For now, we simply summarise that, at least under overly idealised conditions, the methods presented here can be shown to converge, and we leave the study of non-manifold carré du champ convergence for future work.

We defined spanning sets like φᵢdxⱼᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ d.
It is essential that the immersion coordinates xⱼ are smooth enough because of the issue of carré du champ estimation.
The largest possible set we could work with is eᵢdeⱼᵢ,ⱼ≤ ₙ for pointwise basis functions eᵢ (or, by a basis change, φᵢdφⱼᵢ,ⱼ≤ ₙ), but this would include computing the derivatives of functions that are too irregular.
We could interpret the smaller spanning set φᵢdxⱼᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ d merely as a computational cost reduction, or as an opportunity to inject inductive bias through the choice of xⱼ, but this analysis reveals that picking smooth xⱼ is essential to maintain sufficient accuracy.

In a similar vein, if we set n₁=n, then computations like d(φᵢdxⱼ)=dφᵢdxⱼ would involve the derivatives of overly irregular φᵢ.
This means that, for any operators that involve derivatives, the choice of n₁≪ n is also essential in constructing a kind of ‘empirical Sobolev space’ of forms on which the derivative is reasonably accurate.

# 5 Differential operators

Differential operators, like the exterior derivative and Lie bracket, generalise the standard vector calculus methods from ℝ³ to more general spaces.
They are fundamental in the theory of Riemannian geometry on manifolds, and diffusion geometry generalises them to more general spaces.
We can apply the method of weak formulations to compute discrete representations of differential operators from diffusion geometry, in order to apply them in data analysis.

[anchor: S5.SS1]
## 5.1 Gradient and divergence

The gradient is a map ∇:A→X(M).
If f∈A is a function, its gradient ∇ f is a vector field that measures the direction and rate of steepest increase of f.
We discretise it as an n₁d× n₀ matrix ∇ that maps functions in ℝⁿ₀ to vector fields in ℝⁿ₁ᵈ.
We obtain this via a weak formulation ∇ʷᵉᵃᵏ that computes the inner product of ∇(φᵢ) and φᵢ′∇ xⱼ′, in the 3-tensor
    ∇ʷᵉᵃᵏᵢ′ⱼ′ᵢ=ᵢ′∇ xⱼ′,∇φᵢ=∫φᵢ′Γ(xⱼ′,φᵢ)dμ=Σₚ₌₁ⁿUₚᵢ′Γₚ(xⱼ′,φᵢ)μₚ,

which has dimension n₁× d× n₀.
We reshape ∇ʷᵉᵃᵏ into an n₁d× n₀ matrix ∇ʷᵉᵃᵏI′ᵢ, the weak gradient operator, and recover the strong operator ∇:ℝⁿ₀→ℝⁿ₁ᵈ as ∇=(G⁽¹⁾)⁺∇ʷᵉᵃᵏ.

## 5.3 Directional derivatives

Vector fields are first-order differential operators and act on functions by X(f)=g(X,∇ f), for f∈A and X∈X(M), which we can interpret as the directional derivative of f in the direction of X.
If f∇ h∈X(M) is a vector field and f′∈A is a function, then we can evaluate
    (f∇ h)(f′)=fg(∇ h,∇ f′)=fΓ(h,f′)∈A.

Now taking
    X=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼφᵢ∇ xⱼ

we see that, if f∈A is a function, then
    X(f)=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼφᵢΓ(xⱼ,f).

We previously used this fact to visualise vector fields by applying them to the coordinate functions xⱼ (subsection 3.3.3), but will now compute the action of a vector field as an operator on functions via a weak formulation.
Any vector field X∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ will correspond to a linear operator on functions, which is represented by an n₀× n₀ matrix Xᵒᵖ, which we can access via its weak formulation Xᵒᵖ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ.
We compute the action of X on the function φₜ via its inner product with φₛ in the n₀× n₀ matrix
    Xₛₜᵒᵖ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ =ₛ,X(φₜ) =Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼₛ,(φᵢ∇ xⱼ)(φₜ) =Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼₛ,φᵢΓ(xⱼ,φₜ) =Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈXᵢⱼ∫φₛφᵢΓ(xⱼ,φₜ)dμ  =Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₁Σⱼ₌₁ᵈΣₚ₌₁ⁿXᵢⱼUₚₛUₚᵢΓₚ(xⱼ,φₜ)μₚ.

We recover Xᵒᵖ as Xᵒᵖ=(G⁽⁰⁾)⁺Xᵒᵖ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ.
In the standard case where the function basis is orthonormal, G⁽⁰⁾=Iₙ₀ so Xᵒᵖ=Xᵒᵖ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ, but this general approach will work for any function basis.
So, if f∈ℝⁿ₀ is a function and X∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ is a vector field, then X(f)=Xᵒᵖf∈ℝⁿ₀.

## 5.4 Hodge Laplacian

The Hodge Laplacian is the map Δ⁽ᵏ⁾=∂⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾d⁽ᵏ⁾+d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾∂⁽ᵏ⁾:Ωᵏ(M)→Ωᵏ(M) which generalises the Laplacian on functions.
It has the powerful property of measuring the topology of the space through its spectrum: see Section 8.
We will discretise Δ⁽ᵏ⁾ as a n₁dk× n₁dk matrix Δ⁽ᵏ⁾ that acts on k-forms in ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ.
We will compute the weak formulation (which is called the Hodge energy)
    ,Δ⁽ᵏ⁾β=d⁽ᵏ⁾α,d⁽ᵏ⁾β+∂⁽ᵏ⁾α,∂⁽ᵏ⁾β

as an n₁dk× n₁dk matrix Δ⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ and recover Δ⁽ᵏ⁾=(G⁽ᵏ⁾)⁺Δ⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ.
If we only want to know the spectrum of Δ⁽ᵏ⁾, we can efficiently solve the generalised eigenproblem
    Δ⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏα=λG⁽ᵏ⁾α,

which is fully symmetric because Δ⁽ᵏ⁾ is self-adjoint.
To compute the Hodge energy Δ⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ, we address the ‘up’ and ‘down’ energy terms separately.
We can write the ‘down’ energy ∂⁽ᵏ⁾α,∂⁽ᵏ⁾β as
    ∂⁽ᵏ⁾α,∂⁽ᵏ⁾β =,d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾∂⁽ᵏ⁾β =αG⁽ᵏ⁾d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾∂⁽ᵏ⁾β  =αG⁽ᵏ⁾((G⁽ᵏ⁾)⁺d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)((G⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾)⁺(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀ)β  =αd⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ(G⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾)⁺(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀβ,

so the ‘down’ energy has matrix representation
    Down⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ=d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ(G⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾)⁺(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀ

which is the weak formulation of the down Laplacian d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾∂⁽ᵏ⁾.
We could compute the ‘up’ energy in the same way as
    Up⁽ᵏ⁾=(d⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀ(G⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾)⁺d⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ

but this involves mapping explicitly from ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ into the possibly larger space ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ⁺¹ and inverting the n₁dk+1× n₁dk+1 matrix G⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ there, which would be computationally expensive.
Instead, we employ the kernel trick and directly evaluate the ‘up’ energy only on k-forms in ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ.
We compute the inner product of d⁽ᵏ⁾(φᵢdx₍J₎) and d⁽ᵏ⁾(φᵢ′dx₍J′₎) (with J=(j₁<…<jₖ) and J′=(j′₁<…<j′ₖ)) and collect these into the 4-tensor
    Up⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏᵢ′J′ᵢJ =d⁽ᵏ⁾(φᵢ′dx₍J′₎),d⁽ᵏ⁾(φᵢdx₍J₎)
    =dφᵢ′dxⱼ′₁…dxⱼ′ₖ,dφᵢdxⱼ₁…dxⱼₖ
    =∫detΓ(φᵢ′,φᵢ) Γ(φᵢ′,xⱼ₁) … Γ(φᵢ′,xⱼₖ)
    Γ(xⱼ₁′,φᵢ) Γ(xⱼ₁′,xⱼ₁) … Γ(xⱼ₁′,xⱼₖ)
    … … … …
    Γ(xⱼₖ′,φᵢ) Γ(xⱼₖ′,xⱼ₁) … Γ(xⱼₖ′,xⱼₖ)
    dμ
    =Σₚ₌₁ⁿdetΓₚ(φᵢ′,φᵢ) Γₚ(φᵢ′,xⱼ₁) … Γₚ(φᵢ′,xⱼₖ)
    Γₚ(xⱼ₁′,φᵢ) Γₚ(xⱼ₁′,xⱼ₁) … Γₚ(xⱼ₁′,xⱼₖ)
    … … … …
    Γₚ(xⱼₖ′,φᵢ) Γₚ(xⱼₖ′,xⱼ₁) … Γₚ(xⱼₖ′,xⱼₖ)
    μₚ,

which has dimension n₁×dk× n₁×dk.
We then reshape Up⁽ᵏ⁾ into an n₁dk× n₁dk matrix Up⁽ᵏ⁾IJ, which is the weak up Laplacian ∂⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾d⁽ᵏ⁾.
Summing the ‘up’ and ‘down’ terms we obtain the Hodge energy
    Δ⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ=Down⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ+Up⁽ᵏ⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ.

Computing the spectrum of the Hodge Laplacian via the weak eigenproblem (38) leads to powerful new tools for topological data analysis, which we will explore in Section 8.

We can exploit the block matrix structure using the Schur determinant identity.

which yields another small improvement.

## 5.5 Hodge decomposition

We can perform the Hodge decomposition of a k-form α∈Ωᵏ(M) into
    α=dₖ₋₁β+γ+∂ₖ₊₁δ,

where β∈Ωᵏ⁻¹(M), γ∈Ωᵏ(M), and δ∈Ωᵏ⁺¹(M), with dₖγ=0 and ∂ₖγ=0.
We call dₖ₋₁β the exact part of α, and γ the harmonic part, and ∂ₖ₊₁δ the coexact part.
We will refer to β as the exact potential of α, and δ as the coexact potential.
The Hodge decomposition is unique, and the three parts are orthogonal.
The harmonic part γ lies in the kernel of the Hodge Laplacian Δₖ.
Intuitively, the harmonic and coexact parts of α both capture its rotational behaviour: the coexact part corresponds to vortices whose centre is in the space, and the harmonic part corresponds to global circular flows around holes in the space.
We can solve for the exact potential β∈Ωᵏ⁻¹(M) using the fact that
    Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖβ=∂ₖdₖ₋₁β=∂ₖ(dₖ₋₁β+γ+∂ₖ₊₁δ)=∂ₖα,

and so β=(Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖ)⁺∂ₖα, where (Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖ)⁺ is the pseudoinverse of Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖ.
In our discretisation, we can rewrite this equation using only weak formulations as
    =(Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖ)⁺∂ₖα
    =((G⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾)⁺Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ)⁺(G⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾)⁺(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀα
    =(Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ)⁺G⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(G⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾)⁺(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀα
    =(Δₖ₋₁ᵘᵖ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ)⁺(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀα,

which we can easily solve.
We likewise compute the coexact potential δ∈Ωᵏ⁺¹(M) as
    δ=(Δₖ₊₁ᵈᵒʷⁿ, ʷᵉᵃᵏ)⁺(d⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾,ʷᵉᵃᵏ)ᵀα,

and obtain the harmonic part of α as γ=α-d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾β-∂⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾δ.
We illustrate the Hodge decomposition of functions and 1-forms in Figure 20.
When f∈A is a function, its harmonic part is locally constant, and its coexact part captures the local variability of f.
The exact part is always zero because there are no (-1)-forms.
When α∈Ω¹(M) is a 1-form, the exact potential is a function f∈A that measures the displacement of the flow along α, and the coexact part contains its vortices.
A 1-form β∈Ω¹(M) on a torus can have a harmonic part that captures a circular flow around the holes in the space.

In practice, the harmonic part γ is always nearly zero.
Any forms that ‘look’ harmonic will have very low Hodge energy, but will not be exactly in the kernel of Δ⁽ᵏ⁾ due to data uncertainty and numerical error.
When we perform their Hodge decomposition, the harmonic behaviour is absorbed into the coexact part, with a very large coexact potential δ.
This applies in the ‘harmonic part’ of the flow on the torus in Figure 20, where the visually harmonic part is actually computed as coexact.
On the other hand, the harmonic part of the function in the top row is genuinely computed as harmonic because of the k-nearest neighbour kernel construction, which makes the computational graph genuinely disconnected.

## 5.6 Lie bracket

The directional derivative of a function f∈A along a vector field X∈X(M) is also called the Lie derivative and is denoted LXf.
This notion extends to vector fields: if X,Y∈X(M) are vector fields, then the Lie derivative of Y along X is another vector field LXY∈X(M) defined by
    LXY(f)=X(Y(f))-Y(X(f))

for any f∈A.
This operation is also called the Lie bracket of X and Y, denoted [X,Y]=LXY, and defines a bilinear map
    [·,·]:X(M)×X(M)→X(M).


Figure 21: Non-commuting flows of two vector fields XX and YY lead to non-trivial Lie bracket [X,Y][X,Y].

We can get some intuition for the Lie bracket by considering what happens on a classical manifold, where it quantifies the failure of the flows generated by X and Y to commute.
Let ΦεX,ΦεY:M→ M denote the infinitesimal flows generated by X and Y, respectively.
Composing them in the opposite order does not, in general, return to the same point:
    ΦY₋ε ΦX₋ε ΦYε ΦXε(p)=p+ε²[X,Y](p)+O(ε³).

The infinitesimal discrepancy ε²[X,Y](p) represents the small closing edge of the flow diagram spanned by X and Y at a point p∈ M: see Figure 21.

In our framework, we discretise the Lie bracket (41) as an n₁d× n₁d× n₁d 3-tensor Lie that maps a pair of vector fields in ℝⁿ₁ᵈ to a vector field in ℝⁿ₁ᵈ.
We compute the weak formulation Lieʷᵉᵃᵏ as the inner product of [φᵢ₁∇ xⱼ₁,φᵢ₂∇ xⱼ₂] and φᵢ′∇ xⱼ′ in the 6-tensor

which has dimension n₁× d× n₁× d× n₁× d.

## 5.7 Hessian

If f:ℝᵈ→ℝ is a smooth function, its Hessian at a point p∈ℝᵈ is the square matrix of second derivatives
    H(f)ₚ=∂²f/∂ x₁∂ x₁(p) … ∂²f/∂ x₁∂ xd(p)
    ⋮ ⋱ ⋮
    ∂²f/∂ xd∂ x₁(p) … ∂²f/∂ xd∂ xd(p)
    ,

which we can think of as a rank-2 tensor field (i.e. a field of matrices) on ℝᵈ.
This is generalised in Riemannian and diffusion geometry to the Hessian operator H:A→Ω¹(M)⊗ ², which maps functions to (0,2)-tensors.
We would like to discretise it as an n₁d²× n₀ matrix H that maps functions in ℝⁿ₀ to 2-tensors in ℝⁿ₁ᵈ².
We can access the Hessian using the carré du champ as
    H(f)(∇ h₁,∇ h₂)=1/2(Γ(h₁,Γ(h₂,f))+Γ(h₂,Γ(h₁,f))-Γ(f,Γ(h₁,h₂))),

which evaluates the action of the Hessian H(f) on the pair of vector fields ∇ h₁ and ∇ h₂ as described in Section 3.5.3.
We will use the weak formulation Hʷᵉᵃᵏ, which we compute as the inner product of φᵢ′dxⱼ′₁⊗ dxⱼ′₂ and H(φᵢ) in the 4-tensor
    Hʷᵉᵃᵏᵢ′ⱼ′₁ⱼ′₂ᵢ =ᵢ′dxⱼ′₁⊗ dxⱼ′₂,H(φᵢ) =∫φᵢ′g(H(φᵢ),dxⱼ′₁⊗ dxⱼ′₂)dμ  =∫φᵢ′H(φᵢ)(∇ xⱼ′₁,∇ xⱼ′₂)dμ  =1/2∫φᵢ′[Γ(xⱼ′₁,Γ(xⱼ′₂,φᵢ))+Γ(xⱼ′₂,Γ(xⱼ′₁,φᵢ))-Γ(φᵢ,Γ(xⱼ′₁,xⱼ′₂))]dμ  =1/2Σₚ₌₁ⁿUₚᵢ′[Γₚ(xⱼ′₁,Γ(xⱼ′₂,φᵢ))+Γₚ(xⱼ′₂,Γ(xⱼ′₁,φᵢ))-Γₚ(φᵢ,Γ(xⱼ′₁,xⱼ′₂))]μₚ,

which has dimension n₁× d× d× n₀.
We reshape Hʷᵉᵃᵏ into an n₁d²× n₀ matrix, and recover the strong formulation H:ℝⁿ₀→ℝⁿ₁ᵈ² as H=(G⁽⁰,²⁾)⁺Hʷᵉᵃᵏ.

The Hessian is a symmetric tensor field, and so we can implement an optimised version that only computes the d(d+1)/2 unique components of the d× d Hessian matrix at each point, and maps directly into the smaller space of symmetric 2-tensors Ω¹(M)⊗ ², ˢʸᵐ described in Computational Note 3.5.2.

We can compute the action of the Hessian Hf∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ² on any pair of vector fields X,Y∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ using the method described in Section 3.5.3, which represents the second derivative of f in the directions X and Y.
We illustrate the Hessian of functions on 2d and 3d data in Figure 23.
In the bottom row, we compute the Hessian of the z coordinate function, which measures the curvature of the surface in the vertical direction.
This is explicitly connected to the curvature of the space through the second fundamental form, which we exploited to compute the various curvature tensors in 52.

This approach to computing the Hessian as a bilinear operator X(M)×X(M)→A is indirect, because it passes via an expansion in the φᵢdxⱼ₁⊗ dxⱼ₂ basis for the (0,2)-tensors.
Alternatively, we could directly implement the bilinear operator form, which we describe in the Appendix.
This latter approach may be more direct, but the indirect approach we favour here is more extrinsic and creates the opportunity for regularisation through the immersion coordinate functions.

One application of the Hessian operator is when we are given k vector fields X₁,…,Xₖ and compute the k× k matrix of second derivatives [H(f)(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)]ᵢ,ⱼ≤ ₖ.
This can be used for classifying the critical points of a function, as we discuss in Section 8.4, where we use this method for Morse theory.

## 5.9 Levi-Civita connection

If f is a function on a manifold, and X∈X(M) is a vector field, let γ:(-ε,ε)→ M be a smooth curve with γ(0)=x and γ′(0)=X(x).
We can define the directional derivative of f along X as
    X(f)=∇X(f):=limₜ→ ₀f(γ(t))-f(γ(0))/t,

which depends only on the values of f along the path γ, and not on its neighbouring values.
If we try to naively extend this definition to a directional derivative of a vector field Y, we find that
    ``∇X(Y):=limₜ→ ₀Y(γ(t))-Y(γ(0))/t"

is not well-defined.
Indeed, Y(γ(t)) and Y(γ(0)) belong to different tangent spaces, and generally, there is no canonical way to identify tangent spaces.Covariant derivative operators remedy this by providing a consistent way to compare tangent spaces, and there are many possible choices of covariant derivative that all encode different geometric information, such as curvature.

Among all covariant derivatives, the Levi-Civita connection is distinguished: it is the metric-compatible, torsion-free connection naturally associated with a Riemannian metric g, and the one we will compute in diffusion geometry.
The Levi-Civita connection ∇XY is a vector field that measures the infinitesimal change of Y in the direction of X by parallel transporting Y back along the flow of X, preserving the lengths and angles defined by the metric g.

In diffusion geometry, the Levi-Civita connection is defined as an operator ∇:X(M)→Ω¹(M)⊗ ² that maps vector fields to (0,2)-tensors.
We define ∇XY as the operator form of the (0,2)-tensor ∇ Y applied to the vector field X (see Section 5.8).
Given a vector field X=Σᵢ,ⱼφᵢ∇ xⱼ, we define ∇ X as
    ∇ X:=Σᵢ,ⱼ(dφᵢ⊗ dxⱼ+φᵢH(xⱼ)).

We can discretise ∇ as an n₁d²× n₁d matrix ∇ that maps vector fields in ℝⁿ₁ᵈ to 2-tensors in ℝⁿ₁ᵈ².
We will use the weak formulation ∇ʷᵉᵃᵏ, which we compute as the inner product of ∇(φᵢ′∇ xⱼ) and φᵢdxⱼ₁⊗ dxⱼ₂ in the 5-tensor
    ∇ᵢⱼ₁ⱼ₂ᵢ′ⱼ′ʷᵉᵃᵏ =ᵢ′dxⱼ′₁⊗ dxⱼ′₂,∇(φᵢ∇ xⱼ)
    =ᵢ′dxⱼ′₁⊗ dxⱼ′₂,dφᵢ⊗ dxⱼ+φᵢH(xⱼ)
    =∫φᵢ′Γ(xⱼ′₁,φᵢ)Γ(xⱼ′₂,xⱼ)dμ+∫φᵢ′φᵢH(xⱼ)(∇ xⱼ′₁,∇ xⱼ′₂)dμ
    =Σₚ₌₁ⁿUₚᵢ′Γₚ(xⱼ′₁,φᵢ)Γₚ(xⱼ′₂,xⱼ)μₚ
    +1/2Σₚ₌₁ⁿUₚᵢUₚᵢ′[Γₚ(xⱼ′₁,Γ(xⱼ′₂,xⱼ))+Γₚ(xⱼ′₂,Γ(xⱼ′₁,xⱼ))-Γₚ(xⱼ,Γ(xⱼ′₁,xⱼ′₂))]μₚ,

using an adaptation of the weak formulation of the Hessian computed above.
This weak formulation 5-tensor has shape n₁× d× d× n₁× d, which we reshape into a n₁d²× n₁d matrix.
We can compute the strong formulation as ∇=(G⁽⁰,²⁾)⁺∇ʷᵉᵃᵏ, which represents ∇ as a linear map ℝⁿ₁ᵈ→ℝⁿ₁ᵈ².
We can compute the operator form of the Levi-Civita connection Y↦∇XY using the method described in Section 5.8, which represents ∇X as a linear operator ℝⁿ₁ᵈ→ℝⁿ₁ᵈ.

We visualise the Levi-Civita connection in Figure 24.
To verify the computation, we compute examples on spaces where the connection can be evaluated analytically.
In flat 2d space, if X=∇ x and Y=x∇ y, we expect ∇X(Y)=∇ y, and if rot=x∇ y-y∇ x, then ∇ᵣₒₜ(rot)=-1/2∇(x²+y²).

## 5.10 Comparison between Lie bracket and Levi-Civita connection

The Lie derivative LX=[X,·] and the Levi-Civita connection ∇X both define a notion of the ‘derivative of one vector field with respect to another’, and they both generalise the directional derivative of functions f↦ X(f) to vector fields.
However, they do so in different ways.

The Levi-Civita connection ∇XY is a point operator in X, meaning the operator ∇X at a point p only depends on the pointwise evaluation Xₚ.
In particular, if X′ is a different vector field such that X′ₚ=Xₚ, then (∇XY)ₚ=(∇X′Y)ₚ for any vector field Y.
This insensitivity to the neighbouring values of X makes the Levi-Civita connection the true generalisation of the directional derivative (which is also a point operator) to vector fields.

On the other hand, the Lie derivative measures the failure of commutativity of the flows.
It therefore depends on the behaviour of the fields in a neighbourhood of the point.
This sensitivity arises from the fact that the Lie bracket satisfies the Leibniz rule in both arguments, so its value at a point cannot be determined from pointwise data alone.
In Figure 25, we compute examples of the difference.

# 6 Differential equations

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are ubiquitous in mathematical modelling for flows and dynamical processes.
When the domain of those equations is spatial or otherwise geometric, the geometry of the underlying space will affect the dynamics.
For example, an electrical wave travelling along the surface of the brain will speed up and slow down depending on the curvature of the surface at each point.
Spatial PDEs are usually solved using mesh-based methods, where the space is known in advance and explicitly triangulated, but these are hard to apply to observed spatial data in the form of point clouds.
We can apply diffusion geometry as a mesh-free method for solving a wide variety of spatial PDEs, by using our new computational scheme to represent differential operators.

## 6.1 First-order differential equations

We first consider differential equations that are first-order in t, like
    ∂ uₜ/∂ t=T(uₜ)

for a linear differential operator T, with some initial condition u₀=f.
In our discretisation, functions uₜ,f∈A are represented by vectors uₜ,f∈ℝⁿ₀, and linear operators T:A→A by n₀× n₀ matrices T.

with u₀=f.

### 6.1.2 Vector field flows

If X∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ is a vector field, then Xᵒᵖ∈ℝⁿ₀× ⁿ₀ is the square matrix that describes the action of X on functions f∈ℝⁿ₀ (i.e. X(f)=Xᵒᵖf, see 5.3).
The equation
    ∂uₜ/∂ t=X(uₜ)

with initial condition u₀=f describes the ‘flow’ of f along X, and is solved by setting T=Xᵒᵖ.

On smooth (not necessarily Riemannian) manifolds, a vector field X induces a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms Φₜ, which flow points along the integral curves of X.
In this manifold setting, equation (45) describes the pushforward of a function along Φₜ.

The idea of using the operator form of a vector field to define flows was introduced on meshes in 5.
It has been applied to point cloud data as a form of non-parametric dynamical systems forecasting 151744.

## 6.3 Integral curves

If X∈X(M) is a vector field, then it induces a flow defined by the equation
    dpₜ/dt=X(pₜ),

where pₜ∈ M is the position of a point at time t.
The solution to this ordinary differential equation (ODE) for an initial point p₀ is a path pₜ, called an integral curve, that traces out the vector field from that starting point.
We can compute these curves for all points in M simultaneously by solving the PDEs
    dxᵢ,ₜ/dt=X(xᵢ,ₜ),

where xᵢ,ₜ denotes the iᵗʰ coordinate function at time t, for each i.
This then reduces to solving the vector field flow of X using the method described above in 6.1.2.
Evolving the coordinate functions forward and backwards in t allows us to visualise the one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms induced by X.
We visualise these integral curves in Figure 29.

Figure 29: 
Flow lines of a vector field.
The colour represents flow time.

Changing the local equation (48) into the global one (49) is essential for this method to work.
It would be possible to directly solve equation (48) by representing the point pᵢ as the Dirac delta function eᵢ, but this would be extremely unstable and inaccurate.
Recall that the error of the Laplacian estimation is bounded by the Dirichlet energy of the function it is operating on, and a delta function has ‘infinite energy’.
The global approach of solving for all points simultaneously (49), while superficially less efficient, has a regularising effect from the smoother coordinate functions xᵢ, which have much lower energy.

## 7.1 Geodesic distances

We can apply these tool to approximate the function of geodesic distances from a point p, fₚ:=d(p,·).
Notice that fₚ is 1-Lipschitz, meaning that Γ(fₚ,fₚ)≤ 1 μ-almost everywhere, and that fₚ(p)=0.
Among all the functions that meet these criteria, fₓ has the largest mean value ∫ fₓdμ.
We can therefore characterise the function of geodesic distances from a point x as
    d(p,·)=argmaxf↦∫ fdμ:f(p)=0andΓ(f,f)≤ 1 μ-a.e..

In our discretisation, we represent functions in the eigenfunction basis as vectors f∈ℝⁿ₀, and, in a general choice of function basis, we here suppose that the first function in the basis φ₀=1.
The mean value then satisfies
    ∫ fdμ=f,1=f,φ₀=f₀

and that, if f=Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₀fᵢφᵢ, then
    f(pᵢ)=Σᵢ₌ⱼⁿ₀fⱼφⱼ(pᵢ)=Σⱼ₌₁ⁿ₀fⱼUᵢⱼ=Uᵢf,

where Uᵢ is the 1× n₀ row vector representing ‘evaluation at pᵢ’.
The 1-Lipschitz condition corresponds to Γ(f,f)(pⱼ)≤ 1 for all j=1,...,n, and so is discretised as the collection of n quadratic constraints fᵀΓⱼf≤ 1 for j=1,...,n, where Γⱼ is the n₀× n₀ matrix representing the carré du champ at pⱼ.
We can therefore approximate d(xₚ,·) in our eigenfunction basis as the solution to the convex optimisation problem
    argmax  f₀ subject to  Uₚf=0  fᵀΓⱼf≤ 1forj=1,...,n,

which can be efficiently solved with standard conic programming methods.

Our compressed function space A contains only smooth, low-frequency functions, but the geodesic distance function d(p,·) is not smooth at p (it looks like a cone with a tip at p).
This means that directly approximating d(p,·) in A as stated in (50) will lead to errors near p.
Instead, we model d(p,q)=q-p+f, where q-p is the distance in the ambient space ℝᵈ and f∈A is a non-negative correction.

## 7.2 Curvature

We can measure the curvature of a space with the Riemann curvature tensor, which is the map
    R(X,Y,Z,W) =g(∇X∇YZ,W)-g(∇Y∇XZ,W)-g(∇[X,Y]Z,W)
    =∇(∇YZ)(X,W)-∇(∇XZ)(Y,W)-∇(Z)([X,Y],W),

where the second line directly invokes the action of the Levi-Civita connection as a (0,2)-tensor.
Given a pair of vector fields X,Y∈X(M), the sectional curvature K(X,Y) measures the curvature of the 2-dimensional surface spanned by X and Y, defined by
    K(X,Y)=R(X,Y,X,Y)/X²Y²-g(X,Y)².

It is a symmetric functional K:X(M)×X(M)→A.
We illustrate the sectional curvature in Figure 31.

Figure 31: Sectional curvature of a sphere, torus, and hyperboloid.

Even for data on manifolds, curvature estimation is quite underexplored.
In 52, we computed the scalar, Ricci, and Riemann curvatures for manifold data using the Hessian method described above.
This led to state-of-the-art results for curvature estimation on point clouds, due to the noise robustness of diffusion geometry.
This explicitly used the manifold assumption by working with the second fundamental form, an approach also used in 74 to compute the scalar curvature via local quadratic regression.
The scalar curvature was also computed in 49 via an intrinsic method based on volume comparison.
The use of intrinsic volume estimation makes this suitable for very high ambient dimensions, but less robust and sample efficient.
The sectional curvature presented here is, to our knowledge, the first attempt to compute any of the classical curvature tensors without the manifold assumption.

# 8 Topological data analysis

We now introduce several tools for computing topological features of data.
The topology of a space is a more abstract notion than geometry, and encodes the aspects of the space that are preserved under continuous deformations like stretching or bending.
For example, a sphere and a cube are topologically equivalent, because one can be continuously deformed into the other, even though they are geometrically distinct.

[anchor: S8.SS1]
## 8.1 de Rham cohomology via Hodge theory

A principal tool for measuring the topology of a space M is cohomology, which counts the number of ‘holes’ of different dimensions.
The zeroth cohomology group H⁰(M,ℝ) represents the connected components of M, the first cohomology group H¹(M,ℝ) represents the holes or loops, and the second cohomology group H²(M,ℝ) represents the enclosed spaces or voids inside M.
The sequence continues with higher-dimensional ‘generalised holes’ represented by Hᵏ(M,ℝ) for all k, and, together, represents a kind of coarse-grained topological summary of the space M.

Formally, the de Rham cohomology groups are defined to be
    Hᵏ(M,ℝ)=ker(d⁽ᵏ⁾)/im(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾),

where d⁽ᵏ⁾:Ωᵏ(M)→Ωᵏ⁺¹(M) is the exterior derivative on k-forms.
If α∈ker(d⁽ᵏ⁾), then it is called a closed k-form, and if α∈im(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾), then it is called an exact k-form.
Since the carré du champ gives an inner product on forms, we can identify elements of Hᵏ(M,ℝ), which are cosets like α+im(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾), with their unique minimal element.
This minimal element will be orthogonal to im(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾), or, equivalently, it will be in the kernel of the adjoint of d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾, which is the codifferential ∂⁽ᵏ⁾:Ωᵏ(M)→Ωᵏ⁻¹(M).
We will call such an element a coclosed k-form, and forms that are both closed and coclosed are called harmonic.
We can then identify
    Hᵏ(M,ℝ)≅ker(d⁽ᵏ⁾)∩ker(∂⁽ᵏ⁾)=ker(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾∂⁽ᵏ⁾+∂⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾d⁽ᵏ⁾)=ker(Δ⁽ᵏ⁾),

where Δ⁽ᵏ⁾ is the Hodge Laplacian.
This statement is the Hodge theorem, and tells us that each ‘generalised hole’ in Hᵏ(M,ℝ) corresponds to a unique harmonic form in Ωᵏ(M), and these can be found by computing the kernel of the Hodge Laplacian.
Although there is not yet any formal guarantee that this result extends to more general settings than manifolds, we conjecture that such a connection does exist and will use it to motivate statistics for cohomology based on Hodge theory.

In Section 5.4 we described a method for discretising the Hodge Laplacian Δ⁽ᵏ⁾ as a matrix Δ⁽ᵏ⁾ that acts on k-forms in ℝⁿ₁ᵈᵏ.
The Hodge Laplacian is self-adjoint and positive definite, so it has positive real eigenvalues.
We can compute its spectrum via a generalised eigenproblem (38) to find harmonic (or approximately harmonic) differential forms, which should have zero (or nearly zero) eigenvalue.
In practice, the eigenvalues are never exactly zero, but we observe that eigenvalues that are closer to zero correspond to clearer or larger cohomology classes in the data, and so can be used as a proxy for confidence or size.
Recall that our approach of weak formulations is in the Hilbert space completion L²Ωᵏ(M), and so we are working with the projection of the Hodge Laplacian Δₖ into that space.
As such, we can only hope to approximate the L² cohomology of the space, and will miss out any harmonic forms with unbounded norm.
For example, a punctured disk has a cohomology class representing the missing point at the origin, but this is represented by a harmonic form whose pointwise norm grows like x⁻¹, and so is not in L²Ωᵏ(M) and will not be approximated by this method.
We visualise harmonic forms computed from data in Figures 32 and 33.

Figure 32: de Rham cohomology.
The first three eigenforms of the Hodge Laplacian Δ(1)\Delta^{(1)} are approximately harmonic, and capture the three ‘holes’ in this 2d data (top row).
We can parametrise these holes by circular coordinates, which measure the ‘angle’ of each point around the holes (bottom row).

The most direct relation to the method presented here is from Spectral Exterior Calculus (SEC) 16, which used spectral information from the diffusion maps Laplacian 26 to formulate the Hodge Laplacian on 1-forms, and which we applied for diffusion geometry in 51.
We find that the formulation presented here, with the carré du champ and differential forms generated by the ambient coordinates, leads to more stable eigenforms than SEC.
See the discussion of SEC in Section 10, and further comparison with persistent homology below.

The dimension of the kᵗʰ cohomology group Hᵏ(M,ℝ) is called the kᵗʰ Betti number βₖ, and counts the number of independent k-dimensional holes in M.
The Betti numbers give a topological summary of M, but do not capture everything.
For example, the Betti numbers of the torus are β₀=1, β₁=2, and β₂=1, since the torus is connected, has two independent 1-dimensional holes (the ‘long’ and ‘short’ ways around), and has one enclosed 2-dimensional void inside.
Alternatively, a sphere with two circles attached will also have Betti numbers β₀=1, β₁=2, and β₂=1, since it is also connected, has two independent 1-dimensional holes (the two circles), and has one enclosed 2-dimensional void inside the sphere.
However, these two spaces are topologically distinct, because the two 1-dimensional holes in the torus interact to enclose the 2-dimensional void, but the two 1-dimensional holes in the sphere with circles do not interact at all.

To measure this interaction, we can take two cohomology classes α+im(d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾)∈Hᵏ(M,ℝ) and β+im(d⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾)∈Hˡ(M,ℝ), and compute the wedge product α+im(d⁽ᵏ⁺ˡ⁻¹⁾)∈Hᵏ⁺ˡ(M,ℝ).
This new cohomology class is independent of the choice of representatives α and β, and so induces a well-defined bilinear map on cohomology groups called the cup product

If α∈Hᵏ(M,ℝ) and β∈Hˡ(M,ℝ) are cohomology classes represented by harmonic forms, then their cup product α∪β∈Hᵏ⁺ˡ(M,ℝ) is represented by the harmonic form that is the orthogonal projection of the wedge product α onto ker(Δ⁽ᵏ⁺ˡ⁾).
We can use this to measure the interaction between cohomology classes and tell apart spaces that have the same Betti numbers but different topologies, as we illustrate in Figure 33.

Figure 33: Cup product of cohomology classes.
A torus (top two rows) and a sphere with two circles attached (bottom two rows) both have Betti numbers β0=1\beta_{0}=1, β1=2\beta_{1}=2, and β2=1\beta_{2}=1, so they have two harmonic 1-forms α1,α2\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2} and one harmonic 2-form β\beta, but their topologies are different.
We can tell them apart by computing the cup product α1∪α2\alpha_{1}\cup\alpha_{2}, which is represented by the harmonic part of the wedge product α1∧α2\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}.
We can measure this in the inner product ⟨α1∧α2,β⟩\langle\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2},\beta\rangle, which is large for the torus (0.54) but small for the sphere with circles (0.002).
All the harmonic forms are normalised to have unit norm, and so the inner products are a geometric measure of correlation.

## 8.2 Comparison with persistent homology

The most common method for computing the topology of data is persistent homology 67388421.
Persistent homology turns a point cloud into a combinatorial object called a filtered simplicial complex, whose topology can reveal information about the geometry and topology of the points across different scales.

A standard construction is the Vietoris–Rips filtration, which (approximately) considers balls of radius ε centred at each data point, which will progressively join up as ε increases.
By computing the cohomology of the complex at scale ε, we can measure how many topological features exist that have a radius of at least ε.
As we increase ε from 0 to ∞, each of these features will appear at some birth time b and disappear at a death time d.
The total lifetime d-b is called the persistence of the feature and is typically interpreted as a kind of topological ‘signal strength’.
The pairs (b,d) can be plotted in a 2d scatter plot called a persistence diagram which summarises the overall geometry and topology of the point cloud 84.
We illustrate this construction in Figure 34.

Figure 34: Vietoris-Rips filtration and persistent homology of four points in a square.
At ϵ=0\epsilon=0, four connected components appear, merging at ϵ=1\epsilon=1.
At this scale, a 1-cycle emerges that persists until ϵ=2\epsilon=\sqrt{2}.
The persistence diagram summarises these topological features.
Note that three points overlap in the H0H_{0} diagram.

We note several differences between our approach and persistent homology.

[anchor: S8.I1]
[anchor: S8.I1.i1]
Diffusion geometry is defined on the underlying probability space that the data are sampled from.
Our computed objects are estimators for their continuous counterparts, and so our results, such as the harmonic forms, are inferential statistics.
They are inferring things about the underlying space.
Conversely, persistent homology is only defined for a given filtration on a point cloud, and so is a descriptive statistic.
It is describing the data and is not necessarily an estimator for something about the space.

[anchor: S8.I1.i2]
In diffusion geometry, the harmonic forms provide unique, canonical representatives for each cohomology class.
This means they can be easily visualised, and also vectorised for use in statistics and machine learning.
Conversely, the points in the persistence diagram correspond to multiscale classes of topological features and cannot be canonically visualised or vectorised 1.
This can make diffusion geometry easier to interpret: compare the harmonic forms in Figure 35 to the persistence diagrams.

[anchor: S8.I1.i3]
When the filtration in persistent homology arises from a length scale, such as in Vietoris–Rips, the birth and death times are all lengths in the original data units.
The explicit link between topology and length has led to significant applications in areas where the data’s scale has an essential meaning 69448.
While the harmonic forms in diffusion geometry offer a rich description of the space and can be combined with other calculus tools, they do not assess topological scale in the ambient units.

[anchor: S8.I1.i4]
The wedge product in diffusion geometry leads directly to the cup product structure, as described above.
Conversely, the multiscale aspect of persistent homology comes at the cost of not easily representing the cup product, which does not factor through the persistence diagram decomposition.
There have been analogues of the cup product developed in this setting, like the persistent cup-length and persistent cup-modules 812761, but these come at a high computational cost and do not preserve the clean algebraic structure of the continuous setting.

[anchor: S8.I1.i5]
A practical limitation of topological methods is their computational complexity.
Persistent homology is rarely applied to datasets larger than ≈ 10³ and in degrees bigger than 1.
Diffusion geometry is more efficient in both computational time and memory, by several orders of magnitude, as we explore in Section 9.

### 8.2.1 Stability and robustness

Two fundamental properties of geometric and topological statistics are their stability to perturbation and their robustness to noise.
We say that a statistic of a point cloud is stable if small changes to the input point cloud correspond to small changes in the statistic (with respect to some metric on each).
Conversely, a statistic is robust if its value only changes slightly when the input data are noisy.
This is not a precise notion because it entirely depends on the type of noise, but it is generally understood to include large perturbations and outliers.

The stability theorem guarantees the stability of persistent homology with respect to the bottleneck distance on the diagrams, which treats the diagram as a multiset 25.
The computations in this paper all involve matrices and tensors, whose entries depend smoothly on their input, and so are smooth.
The solutions to weak formulations will therefore vary smoothly.
The solutions to the generalised eigenproblems (which we use to find harmonic forms) are more subtle.
In general, the eigenvalues will vary continuously, which is the most direct analogue of the persistent homology stability theorem.
The eigenvectors, however, are not stable, because when two eigenvalues change places, the eigenvectors will jump discontinuously from one to the other (a problem mirrored in the computation of cocycle representatives in persistent homology).
However, provided the eigenvalues are all distinct, then, for sufficiently small perturbations, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are both smooth.

In diffusion geometry, the underlying computation uses a heat kernel which smoothly aggregates the different data points, so our harmonic forms are extremely robust to both large perturbations and outliers.
Conversely, the Vietoris-Rips filtration is much more easily distorted, and is not significantly robust: see Figure 35.

When the Gram matrix has small eigenvalues, and we employ regularisation (see Section 4), the stability of the solutions will depend on the regularisation.
They will be stable for Tikhonov regularisation, but not generally for spectral cutoff.

## 8.3 Circular coordinates for 1-cohomology classes

Given a harmonic 1-form α∈Ω¹(M), we can ask how far a given point p∈ M is around the corresponding 1-dimensional hole.
When M is a manifold, then, under certain conditionson α, this can be measured by a circular coordinate, which assigns an angle θ(p)∈ S¹ to each point p.
Locally, θ satisfies α=2π dθ, and so we can interpret the flow generated by the dual vector field α as pushing points around the hole at a constant angular speed.
We will compute candidate circular coordinates for a 1-form α using the heuristic that the flow generated by α is purely rotational.

[anchor: S8.Thmtheorem1]
If α∈Ω¹(M) is a 1-form, then its dual vector field α has adjoint (α)*=∂α-α with respect to the inner product on L²(μ).
In particular, ∂α=0 if and only if α is skew-adjoint, so it has a purely imaginary spectrum.

[anchor: S8.SS3.1]
We calculate
    (f),hL²₍μ₎=g(α,df),hL²₍μ₎=∫ hg(α,df)dμ=∫ g(hα,df)dμ=hα,dfΩ¹₍M₎.

Using the fact that ∂=d*, this equals
    ∂(hα),fL²₍μ₎=h∂(α)-g(α,dh),fL²₍μ₎=h∂(α)-α(h),fL²₍μ₎

for any f,h∈A.
∎

If ∂α=0 (or, equivalently, its Hodge decomposition has no exact part), we say that α∈Ω¹(M) is coclosed.
Harmonic forms are coclosed, so their dual vector fields are skew-adjoint, and their flows are rotational.
Given a harmonic 1-form α∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ, we can compute αᵒᵖ, the operator form of its dual vector field, as described in Section 5.3.
If we diagonalise this matrix, we expect it to have imaginary eigenvalues λᵢ and complex eigenfunctions zᵢ∈ℂⁿ₀ that evolve under the flow of αᵒᵖ as zᵢ↦ eλᵢᵗzᵢ.
Using the heuristic that the flow of α rotates uniformly around the hole, we will use the circular functions arg(zᵢ)∈ S¹ with the smallest eigenvalue magnitudes |λᵢ| as candidate circular coordinates for α.

In practice, we regularise the vector field αᵒᵖ by adding a Laplacian term to get
    Lε=αᵒᵖ-εΔ,

where higher values of ε give smoother eigenvectors.
When ε is too high, the eigenvalues of Lε eventually become purely real valued, and when ε is too low, the eigenfunctions become too irregular to be useful.
In all our examples, we set ε=1 and choose the complex eigenfunction with the smallest eigenvalue magnitude.
We plot these coordinates next to their corresponding harmonic forms in Figures 32 and 33.
We stress that the idea of using the flow of a coclosed 1-form to generate circular coordinates is purely heuristic, and we do not offer any formal guarantees.
The fact that it works robustly in all our examples raises several interesting theoretical questions for future work.

The idea of using 1-forms to generate circular coordinates on data was introduced in 32, which used persistent cohomology to find integral 1-forms and then solved a least-squares problem to find circular coordinates, and was developed in 65.
The idea of smoothing the vector field by adding a diffusion term is a standard trick in dynamical systems analysis, such as 44.

## 8.4 Morse theory

Another method from differential topology that we can access with diffusion geometry is Morse theory, which uses the critical points of functions to measure the topology of a space.
If f:M→ℝ is a smooth function on a manifold M, then a point p∈ M is a critical point of f if ∇ f vanishes there.
At a critical point p, the Hessian H(f)(p):X(M)×X(M)→ℝ is a bilinear form that measures the second derivatives of f at p, in the direction of a pair of vector fields.
If M is D-dimensional, then H(f)(p) has rank at most D.
We call a critical point p non-degenerate if H(f)(p) has rank D, and a function f is a Morse function if all its critical points are non-degenerate.
If p is a non-degenerate critical point of f, then the Morse index of f at p is the number of negative eigenvalues of H(f)(p), which is an integer between 0 and D.
As such, the Morse index measures the number of independent directions around p along which f is locally concave, and so is 0 at local minima, D at local maxima, and some integer in between at saddle points.
The Morse indices, and, more generally, the eigenvectors of the Hessian, give a complete topological description of the underlying manifold M.

We can compute these indices using our diffusion geometry framework.
First, we can find critical points of a function f∈A from the norm of its gradient ∇ f, which is (approximately) zero at critical points.
We can also compute the Hessian H(f)∈Ω¹(M)⊗ ², and illustrate these all in Figure 36.

Figure 36: Function derivatives and critical points.
If f∈𝒜f\in\mathcal{A} is a function, then ∇f∈𝔛​(M)\nabla f\in\mathfrak{X}(M) measures the velocity of ff and is zero at critical points.
In the third panel, we plot an indicator function exp⁡(−‖∇f‖)\exp(-\|\nabla f\|) that highlights the critical points of ff.
The Hessian H​(f)∈Ω1​(M)⊗2H(f)\in\Omega^{1}(M)^{\otimes 2} measures the second derivatives of ff (its acceleration) in the direction of a pair of vector fields.

If p is a critical point of f and X₁,...,XD∈X(M) are any D linearly independent vector fields at p, then we can represent H(f)(p) as a D× D symmetric matrix with entries H(f)(p)ᵢⱼ=H(f)(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)(p).
Let G(p)=(g(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)(p))ᵢ,ⱼ₌₁ᴰ be the Gram matrix of the vector fields at p, which is strictly positive definite because the Xᵢ are linearly independent.
Then the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of H(f)(p) are solutions to the generalised eigenproblem
    H(f)(p)v=λ G(p)v.

Notice that these solutions are independent of the choice of vector fields Xᵢ, since changing the vector fields corresponds to a change of basis in ℝᴰ.
This eigenproblem is fully symmetric, and so has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors with respect to G(p).
The positive eigenvalues correspond to directions along which f is locally convex, and the negative eigenvalues correspond to directions along which f is locally concave.
We can therefore compute the Morse index of f at p by counting the number of negative eigenvalues of this generalised eigenproblem.

# 9 Computational complexity and scaling

We now summarise the computational complexity of the methods introduced in this paper, and measure empirical scaling laws.

[anchor: S9.SS1]
## 9.1 Complexity

In our proposed framework, the complexity depends most significantly on the number of data points n and the immersion dimension d.
As we discussed in Computational Note 3.3, the immersion coordinates we use are arbitrary, and their number need not be the ambient dimension.
We therefore assume that d is of the order of the intrinsic dimension and so, like n, is an essential attribute of the data.

Given these constraints, we have several tools to control the computational complexity, which can be tuned to suit the available hardware and desired numerical precision.
These parameters are the nearest-neighbour kernel sparsity k, the dimension of the compressed function space n₀, and the number of coefficient functions n₁ used to compute the vector fields, forms, and tensors.

## 9.2 Scalability

In Section 8.1, we described a method for topological data analysis based on de Rham cohomology, which involves computing and diagonalising the Hodge Laplacian (see Section 5.4).
We will use this as a test of the scalability of diffusion geometry by measuring empirical scaling laws against the dataset size n and dimension d.
As a reference, we will compare the scaling performance of diffusion geometry with Ripser 11, which is the state-of-the-art software for Vietoris-Rips persistent homology (see Section 8.2 for further comparisons).

We note two differences between our method and persistent homology.
[anchor: S9.I1]
[anchor: S9.I1.i1]
Computing persistent homology in degree k requires also computing it in all the degrees 0,...,k-1.
Diffusion geometry can isolate degrees.

[anchor: S9.I1.i2]
The complexity of persistent homology in degree k is (in the worst case) O(n³ᵏ⁺³).
In diffusion geometry, the Hodge Laplacian on k-forms is an n₁dk× n₁dk matrix, and computing it costs O(nn₁²dk²).
In particular, the complexity does not depend significantly on the degree k as a function of n, even though it will be greater when dk is large.


To give a realistic assessment of these methods’ usability in practical data science contexts, we perform all the tests on standard consumer hardware (a 2024 MacBook Pro with 24GB memory).

# 10 Background and context

Since this paper covers a wide range of topics, we have discussed some related work in each section as the need arises.
Here, we provide some general background for diffusion geometry in the context of computational geometry and topology.

[anchor: S10.SS1]
## 10.1 Theory

Diffusion geometry is formulated using the language of Bakry-Émery Γ-calculus 78, which studies Markov diffusion processes through a bilinear operator called the carré du champ Γ.
In diffusion geometry, the carré du champ is interpreted as a generalisation of the Riemannian metric, allowing Riemannian geometry to be generalised to any space with a diffusion process.
There have been many other approaches to generalising Riemannian geometry beyond manifolds, which are mostly purely theoretical, but a notable exception is 10, which introduces a Hodge theory on metric measure spaces that can be computed using a simplicial complex on the data (see the following discussion of combinatorial models).
For a general theoretical background of diffusion geometry, see 51.

[anchor: S10.SS2]
## 10.2 Topological models

The dominant approach to computational geometry and topology is to construct a combinatorial structure on the data, such as a graph, simplicial complex, hypergraph, or related object.
In this model, functions are represented as values on vertices, vector fields and differential 1-forms as values on edges, and so on.
The combinatorial structure can be used to compute discrete analogues of differential operators, such as the graph Laplacian or the combinatorial exterior derivative.

These ideas originate from Whitney 80, who used simplicial complexes to approximate differential forms on manifolds, and Dodziuk 3534, who introduced combinatorial Hodge theory.
They later became widely applied in computational geometry and topology, with applications in computer graphics and simulation, and were formalised in the theory of discrete exterior calculus 5033.
This framework can represent many of the standard objects from vector calculus and Riemannian geometry, such as vector fields, differential forms, the exterior derivative, Hodge star, and Laplacian.
There has been some progress towards representing more general tensors like the Hessian 23, connection 19, and Lie bracket 31, although these require special assumptions and fit less naturally into the framework.
Although these methods were originally developed for manifolds, they do not require any kind of tangent space basis, so they can be used on any combinatorial complex.
A notable application is in topological data analysis, which we will address in Section 8.
As we discussed above, the fundamental objects of interest in geometry and calculus are differential operators, and a central question in discrete exterior calculus is how to represent these operators in a way that is stable under refinements of the complex and will eventually converge to the true operators on the underlying space.
This was resolved by finite element exterior calculus (FEEC) 3224, which used ideas from numerical analysis, specifically applying the Galerkin method to solve PDEs using weak formulations.
Although our model is not combinatorial, we will take exactly this approach to define differential operators in Section 5.

However, there are several limitations to applying combinatorial models to point cloud data.
First, it is hard to construct a suitable complex on the data in a statistically robust way.
These methods were generally developed for applications where the complex is given, and the results tend to depend sensitively on the choice of complex if the input data is just a point cloud, with small changes in the data leading to large changes in the complex (see the discussion in 5152).
Second, combinatorial complexes grow very quickly in size as the number of data points increases, making them computationally expensive.
Third, and most fundamentally, combinatorial models are essentially topological and not geometric.
They have an exterior derivative that obeys the Leibniz rule, leading to meaningful cohomology groups with a cup product, and so are good models for topology.
However, to model Riemannian geometry, we would like to define a Riemannian metric g, whose integral over the space defines a global inner product.
If g is a pointwise inner product, then it must satisfy
    fg(X,Y)=g(f· X,Y)=g(X· f,Y)=g(X,f· Y)=g(X,Y· f)

for all functions f and vector fields X,Y, where · denotes the multiplication of functions and vector fields.
If X,Y=∫ g(X,Y) is an inner product (for some notion of integral, such as a weighted sum over the vertices), then multiplication must be both associative and commutative, which in these combinatorial models it is not.
As such, combinatorial models cannot have a Riemannian metric.

[anchor: S10.SS3]
## 10.3 Geometric models

The alternative class of models are those that admit a Riemannian metric, which we will call geometric models.
Most geometric models represent vector fields, forms, and tensors as pointwise vectors, and so are more aligned with the continuous notion of vector bundles.
For example, given n data points in ℝᵈ, a vector field could be represented as an n× d matrix, where each row is a d-dimensional vector representing the vector field at that data point.
This system easily admits a Riemannian metric by defining a pointwise inner product of these vectors.
Having a Riemannian metric means that geometric models must have an associative and commutative product, and so the vector fields, forms, and tensors are bimodules over the function space.
However, this means they cannot also obey the Leibniz rule, and so are weaker models for topology.

[anchor: S10.SS3.SSS1]
### 10.3.1 Geometric models on a combinatorial complex

Many geometric models still use a combinatorial complex.
When the data lie on a manifold M of dimension d′, the tangent space TₚM at each point p is a d′-dimensional space, and given some choice of basis for each tangent space, we can represent vector fields as nd′-dimensional vectors.
This approach was applied to represent vector fields on triangulated manifolds in computer graphics 6446, where the tangent space at each vertex is approximated by the plane of the adjacent faces.
Provided the tangent spaces at different points can be somehow aligned, this approach can be used to define a discrete parallel transport and vector field diffusion 77792855, as well as the divergence and curl 668341.

An alternative approach to representing a vector field X is to consider its operator form as a directional derivative of functions, i.e. f↦ X(f).
Given a basis for the function space, the vector fields can be identified with the space of square matrices.
This was introduced in 5 for triangulated manifolds, with a function basis given by eigenfunctions of the graph Laplacian.
This choice of basis is a discrete analogue of the Fourier transform of functions, and can be truncated to just the first k eigenfunctions to obtain a low-rank compressed function space of only the smoothest functions.
This approach avoids the need to estimate tangent spaces and leads straightforwardly to certain differential operators like the Lie bracket [X,Y]=XY-YX.
We classify this as a geometric model because the function multiplication is associative and commutative, even though there is not necessarily a Riemannian metric (since X(f)=df(X) does not depend on a metric).
Given a metric, this approach can be used to define the Levi-Civita connection 6.

This combinatorial setting has also seen the application of Bakry-Émery Γ-calculus to define a discrete carré du champ operator using the graph Laplacian 5756.
This has led to the development of a combinatorial Bakry-Émery theory that addresses the discrete analogues of functional inequalities 5340 and geometric bounds 596329.

[anchor: S10.SS3.SSS2]
### 10.3.2 Diffusion methods on point clouds

If the data are given only as a point cloud, then, even if they can safely be assumed to lie on a manifold, it is hard to construct a combinatorial complex on them in a way that the discrete geometry on that complex is statistically robust.
The dominant approach to robust geometry on point clouds is to use methods based on a kernel evaluated on the data, such as a Gaussian kernel.
These approximate the heat diffusion on the underlying space, and approximate the Laplacian operator when that space is a manifold 12267847131842.
While the theoretical justification for these methods has currently only been developed for functions on manifolds, they have been extremely successful in practice when applied to non-manifold data such as images 43 and single-cell RNA data 62.
Diffusion methods have mostly been applied to functions on the data, and less so for geometry (see the diffusion below for some exceptions), but their excellent statistical robustness and computational efficiency have made them popular tools for data analysis.
A notable application is in dimensionality reduction, where the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian are used to construct low-dimensional embeddings of the data 431262.

[anchor: S10.SS3.SSS3]
### 10.3.3 Geometric models on point clouds

Given the limitations of constructing a combinatorial complex on point clouds, a major question is the development of complex-free geometric models for vector fields, forms, and tensors on point clouds.

The most popular approach is to assume that the data lie on a manifold M of dimension d′, and explicitly estimate the tangent spaces TₚM at each point p using local principal component analysis (local PCA).
This gives a representation of vector fields as points in ℝⁿᵈ′, where each d′-dimensional vector represents the vector field at that data point 3954683658.
This same idea can be used to represent the whole tensor algebra, including differential forms and general tensors, by taking the appropriate tensor products of the tangent spaces.
However, it is not clear how to define general differential operators in this framework.
An important step in this direction was vector diffusion maps (VDM) 71, which uses a local alignment of the local PCA tangent spaces to define a parallel transport operator, and combines it with a kernel diffusion between points to define a vector field diffusion process.
The generator of this process approximates the connection Laplacian on vector fields when the data lie on a manifold 7172.
The local PCA tangent spaces can also be used to define other differential operators on functions, such as the Hessian 36.

Just as vector fields on triangulated manifolds can be represented in operator form given a choice of function basis (typically eigenfunctions of the graph laplacian) 5, this idea can also be applied to point clouds (typically using eigenfunctions of the diffusion maps laplacian).
This is particularly popular in dynamical systems, where dynamical vector fields act as directional derivatives on functions 44.

A significant breakthrough in complex-free geometry was Spectral Exterior Calculus (SEC), which builds a discrete representation of vector fields and forms on a manifold using only the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Laplacian 16.
The method is inherently spectral, and can be applied to the diffusion maps Laplacian on point clouds 26 to compute the geometry of manifolds from data.
The authors observed that, if φᵢᵢ≤ ₙ₀ are the first n₀ eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, and we compute the n₀× n₀× n₀ multiplication tensor cᵢⱼₖ=ᵢφⱼ,φₖ described in Computational Note 3.2.2, then we can approximate the Riemannian metric g(∇φᵢ,∇φⱼ) using the carré du champ formula as
    g(∇φᵢ,∇φⱼ)=1/2(φᵢΔφⱼ+φⱼΔφᵢ-Δ(φᵢφⱼ))≈1/2Σₖ₌₁ⁿ₀(λᵢ+λⱼ-λₖ)cᵢⱼₖφₖ,

where λᵢ is the eigenvalue corresponding to φᵢ.
The approximation becomes exact when n₀=n, and in this case the formula is equivalent to applying the discrete carré du champ operator of 57 with the diffusion maps Laplacian 26 to the eigenfunctions φᵢ,φⱼ.
They showed that certain expressions from Riemannian geometry can be expressed via similar formulas involving only the eigenvalues, eigenfunctions, and the multiplication tensor.
This motivated the idea of representing vector fields and forms in an overcomplete spanning set given by the eigenfunctions, such as φᵢ∇φⱼᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ ₙ₂ for vector fields.
This allows the computation of differential operators via a weak formulation in global spaces, just like in FEEC, and is a significant departure from the pointwise construction of tangent spaces in local PCA.
In local PCA, the space of all ambient vector fields on the data ℝⁿᵈ is restricted to the nd′-dimensional subspace of vector fields that lie in the estimated tangent spaces (thereby excluding normal components), which forms a basis.
By contrast, the overcomplete spanning set in SEC does not exclude any normal vectors, but still gives meaningful results because those normal components are simply assigned a very small norm when computing inner products, and so do not contribute significantly in the weak formulations.
In other words, the locally low-dimensional structure of the data is implicit in the Riemannian metric, rather than explicit in the construction of a basis.
The authors applied this method to compute eigenforms of the Hodge Laplacian on 1-forms for data sampled from manifolds.
The SEC demonstrated, among many other things, two important ideas that we build on here.
First, it showed that the discrete carré du champ constructed from a diffusion kernel on the data gives a meaningful estimate of the Riemannian metric.
Second, it showed that, for data on manifolds, local tangent space estimates are not necessary and weak formulations are all you need to compute differential operators, because they use the inner product and that already encodes the necessary information about the geometry.

These properties mean that SEC can be used directly as a tool to compute diffusion geometry, which removes the manifold assumption entirely and defines everything using only the carré du champ.
In 51, we demonstrated this on various non-manifold datasets, and extended the SEC to compute higher order forms, and operators including the metric, derivative, codifferential, wedge product, and Lie bracket, and the SEC has been subsequently applied to represent dynamical systems 30.

However, the SEC has several limitations, which we have sought to address in this work.
First, it relies on computing the full multiplication tensor cᵢⱼₖ in O(nn₀³) time.
This cost can be constrained by taking a small n₀, but then the approximation error of multiplication increases.
Since the spectral approach involves expanding each expression in the eigenfunction basis, this error compounds and leads to the geometric expressions becoming inaccurate.
For example, the inner products are not positive definite, with Gram matrices often having negative eigenvalues of the same order of magnitude as the positive ones, leading to numerical instability.
This can be resolved by taking n₀=n, but then the computational cost of cᵢⱼₖ becomes O(n⁴), which is prohibitively large.

Second, the SEC uses the eigenfunction gradients ∇φⱼ as generators for the spanning set φᵢ∇φⱼᵢ≤ ₙ₁,ⱼ≤ ₙ₂ for vector fields (and the tensor algebra generally).
All we require for ∇ f₁,...,∇ fₘ to generate a spanning set is that the functions f₁,...,fₘ define an immersion into ℝᵐ, and choosing the eigenfunction gradients ∇φⱼ has several drawbacks.
One is that the number of eigenfunctions required to create an immersion depends on the geometric complexity of the data, which we cannot know in advance, and could be arbitrarily large (for example, given multiple connected components).
A second is that the sequence of eigenfunctions will generally cover ‘larger’ geometric features multiple times before addressing smaller ones.
Consider, for example, the unit circle in ℝ² with a small interval attached as [1,1+ε]×0.
The first three eigenfunctions will look like 1, sin(θ), cos(θ) and their gradients 0, cos(θ)∇θ, sin(θ)∇θ will generate the vector fields over the circle, but, if ε is small, it will take a very large number of eigenfunctions before one appears that varies along the small interval.
This will lead to high computational complexity, but also numerical instability, as the space of vector fields over the circle is highly redundant.
By comparison, using the ambient coordinates ∇ x and ∇ y is guaranteed to be always sufficient regardless of the geometric complexity.
In general, there are often better choices of immersion than the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, such as the original ambient coordinates of the data in ℝᵈ, a learned lower-dimensional immersion, or something domain-specific.

Third, the SEC is constrained to access the carré du champ by the spectral formula (51), which depends on the accurate spectral estimation of the Laplacian.
In this work, we have followed the theory of diffusion geometry in treating the carré du champ as a general bilinear operator A×A→A, which can be more easily composed to compute second derivatives, and also provides the flexibility to use different choices of operator.
For instance, we have favoured the mean-centred covariance formula for the carré du champ in 3.1.3.
In general, we could obtain carré du champ operators from other kernel methods or using deep learning to scale these tools to high-dimensional data.
In the theoretical context, the convergence of the weak formulations in this work depends only on the convergence of the carré du champ, which exists in greater generality than the Laplacian, and so may be easier to prove in more general, non-manifold settings.

[anchor: S10.SS4]
## 10.4 Where does diffusion geometry fit?

Our approach to computing diffusion geometry is a geometric model on a point cloud, meaning it has a Riemannian metric, and the function-tensor multiplication is associative and commutative.
We follow the SEC in using a spanning set that is overcomplete, rather than a basis constructed pointwise, to represent vector fields, forms, and tensors.
We follow FEEC and SEC in using weak formulations to define differential operators.
Unlike the SEC, we compute the geometry using a diffusion-based carré du champ operator, with which we can directly evaluate the Riemannian metric, instead of using spectral information from the Laplacian.
In particular, this operator is pointwise positive definite, and so we can define true inner products for vector fields, forms, and tensors.
It also allows us to use any choice of immersion to generate the spanning set, which can encode inductive biases and reduce computational complexity.
By default, we use the original data coordinates in ℝᵈ as the immersion, which we found in 52 to have a strong regularising effect on the geometry, improving robustness to noise and leading to state-of-the-art estimators for curvature.
The central role of the carré du champ operator mirrors its role in the theory of diffusion geometry 51, and so we can directly apply methods like the Bakry-Émery Hessian formula 8 to compute geometric quantities like second derivatives, the Levi-Civita connection, and sectional curvature, as well as higher order tensors.

# 11 Conclusions

Diffusion geometry reformulates classical Riemannian geometry in terms of a Markov process through its carré du champ operator, leading to a simpler and more general theory.
In this work, we have shown that the same principle applies to computation.
We used a heat kernel to compute a carré du champ operator on point clouds, and applied the method of weak formulations to estimate differential operators.
This allowed us to directly translate the theories of vector calculus, Riemannian geometry, geometric analysis, and differential topology into simple, scalable data analysis tools.

# A  Markov chain properties

## Carré du champ as infinitesimal covariance

We now give a proof of Proposition 2.2 from Section 2 about the carré du champ operator for the heat diffusion on ℝᵈ.
The analogous result holds for arbitrary Markov processes, i.e. if Pₜ:A→A is a Markov semigroup with carré du champ operator Γ and transition kernel pₜ(x,dy), then
    (f,h)(p) =limₜ→ ₀1/2t(Pₜ(fh)(p)-Pₜ(f)(p)Pₜ(h)(p))

See 8 for a proof of this general case.

See 2.2

[anchor: Ax1.SSx1.1]
We can Taylor expand
    f(y)=f(x)+(y-x)·∇ f+O(y-x²), h(y)=h(x)+(y-x)·∇ h+O(y-x²),

so that, up to higher order terms,
    (f(y)-f(x))(h(y)-h(x))=((y-x)·∇ f)((y-x)·∇ h).

By standard properties of the trace,
    ((y-x)·∇ f)((y-x)·∇ h)=tr(∇ f(∇ h)→ᵖ(y-x)(y-x)→ᵖ),

so
    ∫ pₜ(p,y)(f(y)-f(p))(h(y)-h(p)) dy
    =2t tr(∇ f(∇ h)→ᵖ)
    =2t ∇ f·∇ h.

Dividing by 2t gives the result.
Finally, since 𝔼[f(X)]-f(p)=O(t) and similarly for h, replacing the expectation by the covariance gives the same limit

as t→ 0.
∎

## Symmetric kernels are self-adjoint Markov chains

We now show that the Markov chains we use are self-adjoint if and only if they are the row-normalisations of symmetric kernel matrices.

[anchor: Ax1.Thmtheorem1]
The following are equivalent:
[anchor: Ax1.I1]
[anchor: Ax1.I1.i1]
P is constructed from a symmetric kernel matrix K by row-normalising,

[anchor: Ax1.I1.i2]
P is reversible with respect to μ, meaning it satisfies the ‘detailed balance’ condition
    μᵢPᵢⱼ=μⱼPⱼᵢ

for all i,j,

[anchor: Ax1.I1.i3]
the linear operator P:L²(A,μ)→ L²(A,μ) is self-adjoint.

If these conditions hold, then μ is a stationary distribution for P.

[anchor: Ax1.SSx2.5]
(1)⟹(2):
Assume that Pᵢⱼ=Kᵢⱼ/Dᵢ where K is symmetric and Dᵢ=Σⱼ₌₁ⁿKᵢⱼ are its row sums, so μᵢ=Dᵢ/ΣₖDₖ.
Then
    μᵢPᵢⱼ=Kᵢⱼ/ΣₖDₖ=Kⱼᵢ/ΣₖDₖ=μⱼPⱼᵢ

so P is reversible.

(2)⟹(1): If P is reversible, we can define a symmetric kernel Kᵢⱼ=μᵢPᵢⱼ.
Let
    Dᵢ=ΣⱼKᵢⱼ=ΣⱼμᵢPᵢⱼ=μᵢ.

Then the Markov chain induced by K isKᵢⱼ/Dᵢ=(μᵢPᵢⱼ)/μᵢ=Pᵢⱼ,
so P is induced by a symmetric kernel.

(2)⟹(3): Assume P is reversible, so μᵢPᵢⱼ=μⱼPⱼᵢ.
Then
    Pf,gμ=Σᵢ(Pf)ᵢgᵢμᵢ=Σᵢ,ⱼPᵢⱼfⱼgᵢμᵢ=Σᵢ,ⱼfⱼgᵢ(μᵢPᵢⱼ)

is symmetric in f and g, so P is self-adjoint as a linear operator L²(A,μ)→ L²(A,μ).

(3)⟹(2):
Notice that
    Peₖ,eₗμ=Σᵢ,ⱼPᵢⱼ(eₖ)ⱼ(eₗ)ᵢμᵢ=Σᵢ,ⱼPᵢⱼδⱼₖδᵢₗμᵢ=Pₖₗμₗ.

If P is self-adjoint, thenPₖₗμₗ=Peₖ,eₗμ=Peₗ,eₖμ=Pₗₖμₖfor all k,l, so P is reversible.

Finally, we see that reversibility implies that μ is a stationary distribution, because
    (μᵀP)ᵢ=ΣⱼμⱼPⱼᵢ=ΣⱼμᵢPᵢⱼ=μᵢ

for all i.
∎

This validates why the symmetric kernel approach is reasonably natural.
We could use a general Markov chain to compute diffusion geometry, and use its stationary distribution as the measure for an L² space of functions, but the heat diffusion operator P would then not be self-adjoint.
The self-adjointness is theoretically expected, and also leads to a computational speedup when we try to find its eigenvectors.

## Operator form of the Hessian

We can interpret the Hessian as a bilinear operator X(M)×X(M)→A via
    H(f)(X,Y):=H(f)(X⊗ Y)=g(H(f),X⊗ Y).

In other words, the ‘operator form’ of the Hessian is a map from A to the bilinear operators X(M)×X(M)→A.
We can discretise this as an n× n₁d× n₁d× n₀ 4-tensor Hᵒᵖ that maps functions in ℝⁿ₀ to bilinear maps ℝⁿ₁ᵈ×ℝⁿ₁ᵈ→ℝⁿ.
We first evaluate the action of H(φᵢ′) on the pair of vector fields φᵢ₁∇ xⱼ₁ and φᵢ₂∇ xⱼ₂ at a point p the 6-tensor
    Hᵒᵖₚᵢ₁ⱼ₁ᵢ₂ⱼ₂ᵢ′ =H(φᵢ′)(φᵢ₁∇ xⱼ₁,φᵢ₂∇ xⱼ₂)(p)  =φᵢ₁(p)φᵢ₂(p)H(φᵢ′)(∇ xⱼ₁,∇ xⱼ₂)(p)  =1/2φᵢ₁(p)φᵢ₂(p)[Γ(xⱼ₁,Γ(xⱼ₂,φᵢ′))(p)+Γ(xⱼ₂,Γ(xⱼ₁,φᵢ′))(p)-Γ(φᵢ′,Γ(xⱼ₁,xⱼ₂))(p)]  =1/2Uₚᵢ₁Uₚᵢ₂[Γ⁽ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁄ᵐⁱˣ⁾ₚⱼ₁ⱼ₂ᵢ′+Γ⁽ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁄ᵐⁱˣ⁾ₚⱼ₂ⱼ₁ᵢ′-Γ⁽ᵐⁱˣ⁄ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁾ₚᵢ′ⱼ₁ⱼ₂]

which has dimension n× n₁× d× n₁× d× n₀.
We reshape Hᵒᵖ into an n× n₁d× n₁d× n₀ 4-tensor, so, if f∈ℝⁿ₀ is a function and X,Y∈ℝⁿ₁ᵈ are vector fields, then
    H(f)(X,Y)=(Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ₀ΣI,J₌₁ⁿ₁ᵈHᵒᵖₚIJᵢXIYJfᵢ)ₚ₌₁,...,ₙ∈ℝⁿ

computes the Hessian of f in the directions X and Y.

[anchor: Ax2.SSx1.SSS0.Px1]
#### Hessian matrix.

If we only want to compute the function H(f)(∇ xᵢ,∇ xⱼ)∈ℝⁿ for some i,j, then we can use the fact that
    H(φᵢ′)(∇xᵢ,∇xⱼ)=(Γ⁽ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁄ᵐⁱˣ⁾ₚⱼ₁ⱼ₂ᵢ′+Γ⁽ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁄ᵐⁱˣ⁾ₚⱼ₂ⱼ₁ᵢ′-Γ⁽ᵐⁱˣ⁄ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁾ₚᵢ′ⱼ₁ⱼ₂)ₚ₌₁,...,ₙ∈ℝⁿ.

At a fixed point p, Γ⁽ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁄ᵐⁱˣ⁾ₚⱼ₁ⱼ₂ᵢ′+Γ⁽ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁄ᵐⁱˣ⁾ₚⱼ₂ⱼ₁ᵢ′-Γ⁽ᵐⁱˣ⁄ᶜᵒᵒʳᵈ⁾ₚᵢ′ⱼ₁ⱼ₂ has dimension d× d× n₀, which, viewed as a linear map ℝⁿ₀→ℝᵈ× ᵈ, maps functions in ℝⁿ₀ to their d× d Hessian matrix at p.
